# Gammapatía monoclonal de significado renal

Neoplasias de células B o plasmáticas  con falla renal, pero no alcanzan criterios diagnósticos de cáncer.

## **Classification of Conditions Involving Monoclonal Gammopathy**

Gammapatía monoclonal → inmunoglobulina monoclonal en plasma, orina o ambos. Más comúnes en proliferación clonal de plasmocitos.

MM = Plasmocitos >10% MO, Peak monoclonal >3 g/dl, daño orgánico (CRAB)

The International Myeloma Working Group added three criteria in 2014 that strongly predict the progression to symptomatic myeloma (>60% bone marrow plasma cells, serum free light-chain ratio >100 with the level of the involved free light chain >10 mg per deciliter, and >1 bone lesion on magnetic resonance imaging).[**2**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r2)

Linfoma linfoplasmocítico (Waldestrom) → se inicia el tratamiento cuando plasmocitos >10% MO y uno de estos: anemia, trombocitopenia, crioglobulinaemia o hiperviscosidad

Smolderin mieloma, o LLC de bajo grado o linfoma linfoplasmocítico de bajo grado → criterios clonales, no tienen daño orgánico.

MGUS, IgM MGUS, linfocitosis monoclonal de linfocitos B → no clonal criteria y no daño orgánico.

Although the diagnostic classification and treatment recommendations are valid for most patients, there are instances that are not covered by the above-mentioned criteria. There may be a discrepancy between the tumor burden and organ (kidney) damage. In multiple myeloma, the kidney impairment criterion in CRAB is limited to kidney injury resulting from cast nephropathy. Light-chain cast nephropathy is now considered a myeloma-defining event because nearly all such cases meet the tumor burden criterion.[**2**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r2) However, kidney lesions other than cast nephropathy do not satisfy the renal impairment criterion for symptomatic myeloma; thus, multiple myeloma cannot be diagnosed in a patient presenting with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition disease (MIDD) unless other CRAB criteria are present.

The criteria for classification become even more confusing when the tumor burden is not met (bone marrow plasma-cell infiltration, <10%). This has led to the inaccurate use of the term “idiopathic,” which has been misinterpreted as benign disease. Moreover, kidney disease is not part of the diagnostic criteria for symptomatic B-cell lymphoma.[**3,4**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r3) Thus, when patients with CLL or lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma present with kidney disease, treatment cannot be recommended unless one of the other criteria is met. Patients who have monoclonal gammopathy–related kidney disease without the requisite tumor burden should not be considered to have a cancer, since the hematologic disorder acts more like MGUS. However, such patients still have a clonal disorder that requires effective treatment.[**8**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r8) The gap in the diagnostic criteria has impeded treatment recommendations for such patients.

## Llenando el vacío diagnóstico

Hasta hace poco, el rol de inmunoglobulinas monoclonales en la falla renal se atribuia solo a los cáncer. 

Pero el daño por Ig monoclonal puede ser pese al origen del tumor.

MGUS no incluye el daño orgánico

MGRS → cualquier neoplasia clonal de células B o plasmática sin criterio de cáncer → produce Ig monoclonal nefrotóxica → con daño renal directo o indirecto

 Las enfermedades renales asociadas a MGRS se caracterizan por tres rasgos: primero, estas enfermedades no responden bien a los regímenes inmunosupresores utilizados en el tratamiento de nefropatías autoinmunes; segundo, los pacientes afectados tienen una tasa muy alta de recurrencia después de un trasplante renal (aproximadamente el 90%) si la gammapatía monoclonal no se elimina antes o inmediatamente después del trasplante; y tercero, los pacientes afectados están en riesgo de progresar al cáncer hematológico correspondiente.

## **Mechanisms of Renal Toxicity from Monoclonal Immunoglobulins**

**Con carga tumoral alta**

→ **nefropatía por cilindros de cadenas ligeras** (Ig >150 mg/dl, unidas a uromodulina por el dominio variable) → obstructiva: no se considera en la gammapatía monoclonal de significado renal

**Con carga tumoral baja**

- **Amiloidosis de caderas ligeras (AL):** Plagamiento incorrecto de la cadena ligera de inmunoglobulina monoclonal → fragmentos amiloides → fibrillas
- **Tubulopatía proximal por cadenas ligeras (LCPT): Cristales intracitoplasmáticos de cadenas ligeras** por mutaciones del dominio variable (VL, son resistentes a proteolisis → alteran lisosomas en TCP → lesión celular → falla reabsorción →  tubulopatía proximal de cadenas ligeras
- **Depósito de cadenas ligeras (LCDD)** o Enfermedad por depósito de inmunoglobulinas monoclonales (MIDD): cadenas monoclonales en el dominio variable tienen dominios hidrofóbicos, glicosilación anormal y cargas positivas → agregación y depósito en mesangio y MBG + túbulos con carga negativa → activan proliferación celular → remodelación matrix extracelular → cambios en células mesangiales
- **Nefropatía por C3:** Ig monoclonal (no solo ligeras) → desregulan vía alterna del complemento por autoanticuerpos anti factor H y otros reguladores → sobreactivación de la vía alterna sistémica o local → depósito de C3
- **Microangiopatía trombótica:** Ig → activación del complemento
- **Cristalglobulinemia:** Ig monoclonales cristalizan → precipitan en arteriolas pequeñas, capilares y glomérulo
- **Glomerulonefritis proliferativa con depósito de inmunoglobulinas policlonales** (PGNMID) → Ig monoclonal en glomérulo → contra antígenos de enfermedades policlonales como PLA2 y cadenas α1(IV) y α2(IV) del colágeno IV → activa complemento → inflamación y proliferación

Las cadenas pesadas monoclonales libres se producen en MIDD cuando una deleción del primer dominio constante de la cadena pesada impide que la cadena pesada se una a la cadena ligera para formar una inmunoglobulina completa y permite la secreción de cadenas pesadas libres por parte de las células plasmáticas. Cuando los crioglobulinas monoclonales se exponen a temperaturas por debajo de la temperatura corporal, pueden precipitarse para formar subestructuras organizadas (microtúbulos o cristales), lo que lleva a la oclusión e inflamación de pequeños arteríolos y capilares dentro de los glomérulos y altera la integridad de la barrera de filtración. Los depósitos microtubulares no crioglobulínicos son característicos de la glomerulonefritis inmunotactoide. Los depósitos de inmunoglobulinas completas también pueden activar la vía clásica del complemento, lo que lleva a inflamación glomerular y proliferación endocapilar.

Monoclonal free heavy chains are produced in MIDD when a deletion of the first constant domain of the heavy chain makes the heavy chain unable to bind the light chain in order to form a whole immunoglobulin and allows secretion of free heavy chains by the plasma cells.[**23**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r23) When monoclonal cryoglobulins are exposed to temperatures below body temperature, they can precipitate to form organized substructures (microtubules or crystals), leading to occlusion and inflammation of small arterioles and capillaries within glomeruli and altering the integrity of the filtration barrier.[**25,26**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r25) Noncryoglobulin microtubular deposits are characteristic of immunotactoid glomerulonephritis.[**27**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r27) Deposits of entire immunoglobulins may also activate the classical pathway of complement, leading to glomerular inflammation and endocapillary proliferation.

## **Kidney Lesions Associated with MGRS**

The majority of kidney diseases associated with MGRS are glomerular disorders, with light-chain proximal tubulopathy and crystal-storing histiocytosis as the exceptions.[**9**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r9) MGRS-related kidney lesions are classified according to the characteristics of the monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits on electron microscopy: organized, nonorganized, or absent deposits ([Figure 2](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#f2)). Organized deposits are further differentiated into fibrils (e.g., in immunoglobulin light-chain–associated amyloidosis [AL amyloidosis]), microtubules (e.g., in immunotactoid glomerulonephritis or cryoglobulinemic glomerulonephritis), and crystals or inclusions (e.g., in light-chain proximal tubulopathy, crystal-storing histiocytosis, or crystalglobulin-induced nephropathy). Nonorganized deposits are observed in MIDD and proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits (PGNMID). Immunoglobulin deposits are absent in C3 glomerulopathy with monoclonal gammopathy and in thrombotic microangiopathy associated with monoclonal gammopathy. The renal and extrarenal manifestations and the pathological and hematologic characteristics of the most common MGRS lesions are provided in [Table 1](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#t1).

## **Diagnosis of MGRS-Related Diseases**

The incidence and prevalence of MGRS-associated diseases are unknown. In Olmsted County, Minnesota, the estimated incidence of MGUS is 7 to 59 times as high as the incidence of glomerular diseases.[**31,32**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r31) Also in Olmsted County, the rate of MGUS is higher among males than among females and increases after the age of 50 years.[**33**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r33) Since the frequency of glomerular diseases also increases with age, it would not be uncommon for patients with kidney disease to have MGUS.

Two independent studies have shown that MGRS-related diseases are present in 40 to 45% of patients with monoclonal gammopathy who undergo a kidney biopsy.[**34,35**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r34) A high urinary protein level (>1.5 g per day), an abnormal serum free light-chain ratio, and microscopic hematuria are associated with MGRS lesions.[**35**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r35) Thus, a kidney biopsy should be considered for patients who have monoclonal gammopathy with these laboratory findings or a rapid loss in kidney function.

Once the diagnosis of MGRS-related disease is made, a hematologic evaluation should be performed to identify the clone that is secreting the pathogenic monoclonal immunoglobulin. Serum and urine protein electrophoresis, immunofixation, and a serum free light-chain assay should be performed if the monoclonal protein has not already been identified.[**36**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r36) A bone marrow biopsy is often needed for clonal identification. The pathologic clone may also be detected in a lymph-node biopsy specimen or by means of peripheral-blood flow cytometry, especially in patients with lymphoma and CLL. Computed tomography, with or without positron-emission tomography, can help to locate focal lesions outside the bone marrow, such as lymph-node or plasma-cell lesions. Plasma-cell clones are most common in patients with AL amyloidosis, MIDD, or light-chain proximal tubulopathy,[**24,37–39**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r24) whereas at least 50% of the clones identified in patients with immunotactoid glomerulonephritis correspond to a CLL lineage.[**25,27**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r25) The odds of finding the pathologic clone are lower among patients who have immunotactoid glomerulonephritis or type I cryoglobulinemic glomerulonephritis. Clonal identification is most difficult in patients who have PGNMID, more than 80% of whom have a negative hematologic evaluation, especially those with monotypic glomerular IgG3 deposits.[**40**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r40) Among detected clones in patients with PGNMID, 50% are derived from plasma cells and 50% correspond to B lymphocytes that express CD20.[**12,40**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r12) In addition to clonal identification, testing of genetic markers such as B-cell lymphoma 2 (*BCL2*) and t(11;14) in bone marrow biopsy specimens may provide additional therapeutic options for selected patients.[**41-43**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r41)

## **Management of MGRS-Related Diseases**

The distinctive pathophysiological features of MGRS call for a management strategy that differs from the strategies used for autoimmune-mediated kidney diseases and hematologic cancers. An understanding of these differences ensures adequate outcomes for affected patients. Whereas treatment of autoimmune-mediated kidney diseases is guided by the renal histopathological findings, treatment of MGRS should be determined by the nature of the clone (B-cell or plasma-cell) that produces the nephrotoxic monoclonal immunoglobulin. For MGRS-related diseases, clone-directed therapy has been found to be superior to the immunosuppressive therapy used in many autoimmune-mediated kidney diseases, such as glucocorticoids, calcineurin inhibitors, mycophenolate mofetil, cyclophosphamide, and low-dose rituximab.[**11,12,44–46**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r11) Favorable outcomes in patients with MGRS, including preservation of renal function, require clone-directed therapy, based on agents that were previously restricted to patients with overt hematologic cancers.[**11,12,24,47–50**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r11) The past reluctance to use such agents for the treatment of MGRS was based on the concern that prolonged use of alkylating agents would confer myelotoxicity, potentially resulting in myelodysplastic syndrome and acute leukemia.[**51,52**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r51) Fortunately, these risks have been mitigated by the availability of newer antimyeloma and antilymphoma therapies with better side-effect profiles, which has transformed the prognosis for patients with MGRS.[**11**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r11)

The goals of treatment also require adjustment for MGRS because most affected patients do not have a life-threatening condition, unless extrarenal organs ([Table 1](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#t1)) are involved (e.g., in most cases of AL amyloidosis).[**9,26,47,53**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r9) The primary objective in the management of MGRS is therefore preservation of kidney function rather than immediate preservation of life,[**47**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r47) and this objective should be considered in balancing the toxic effects of therapy and the potential benefits. In patients who already have advanced CKD, the role of clone-directed therapy should be shifted to reducing the risk of recurrent MGRS after kidney transplantation, since preservation of kidney function is unlikely. In patients who are not candidates for transplantation, treatment should be introduced only if extrarenal involvement is present or the diagnostic criteria for a malignant process are met.

The end points of therapy also differ between MGRS and hematologic cancer. Since nephrotoxicity derives from the monoclonal protein, complete elimination would be ideal, but at a minimum, the monoclonal immunoglobulin level should be substantially reduced. A very good partial hematologic response (defined as a difference between involved and uninvolved free light chain of <4 mg per deciliter or >90% reduction of the involved free light chain) has been shown to be the minimum hematologic response required for preservation of kidney function.[**11,50,54–56**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r11) Some studies suggest that the kidney response can be further improved by achieving extremely strong hematologic responses, such as no minimal residual disease or a reduction of the involved free light chain to less than 2 mg per deciliter.[**53,57**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r53) With plasma-cell clones, this is currently best achieved with bortezomib-based therapy.[**48,58,59**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r48) In patients with MIDD, a more complete hematologic response may be achieved with the use of high-dose melphalan followed by autologous stem-cell transplantation, but not all patients are eligible for the procedure.[**48,60**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r48) Daratumumab, a monoclonal antibody against CD38, has shown high efficacy in patients with AL amyloidosis and is currently being tested for the treatment of MIDD and PGNMID.[**61,62**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r61) This agent would be an outstanding addition to current treatment regimens for plasma-cell clones in patients with MGRS-related diseases if its benefits, including high response rates and relatively low rates of adverse effects, could be confirmed.

Patients with B-cell clones that express CD20 should be treated with rituximab-based therapy.[**11,12,25**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r11) The particular combination with rituximab depends on whether the involved clone is of the CLL, lymphoplasmacytic, or other non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma lineage. Frequent reassessment of the hematologic measures (serum free light-chain level and the presence or absence of a monoclonal spike) and kidney measures (serum creatinine and urinary protein levels) is important in treating MGRS in order to evaluate the response to therapy and minimize toxic effects. Maintenance therapy is generally not necessary, since long-term remission is usually achieved with a limited duration of chemotherapy.[**49,54,59**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r49)

Kidney transplantation is generally the best option if end-stage kidney disease develops. However, high rates of recurrent nephropathy have been reported among patients with MGRS in whom monoclonal gammopathy was not fully eradicated before kidney transplantation. In particular, patients with PGNMID have had a recurrence rate approaching 90% after kidney transplantation, with a median time to recurrence of only 6 months.[**14**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r14) Graft loss has followed quickly after the detection of recurrence in patients who were not rapidly treated. Untreated or insufficiently treated patients with MIDD have had a similar risk of recurrence, but with a longer time to recurrence.[**13,60**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r13) We therefore recommend that patients who are candidates for kidney transplantation be treated until at least a very good partial response is achieved, if a hematologic response can be measured. The only exception is patients with AL amyloidosis, since studies have shown that kidney allograft outcomes in such patients are similar whether they received clone-directed therapy before or after kidney transplantation, as long as at least a very good partial response can be achieved.[**63-65**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r63) This finding is due mainly to the lower risk and slower rate of recurrence among such patients.

There are situations in which a pathologic clone or even monoclonal protein could not be detected to help guide treatment selection, especially in patients with PGNMID. In these circumstances, we have recommended the use of anti–plasma-cell agents in patients with only IgG, IgA, or light-chain monoclonal gammopathy and anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody therapy in those with an IgM monoclonal gammopathy. Treatment has been guided by renal measures such as urinary protein levels and kidney function instead of the hematologic response. If there is no favorable change in urinary protein or serum creatinine levels after two or three cycles, therapy should be changed to target a different clone.

## **Conclusions**

MGRS is a relatively new diagnostic term and concept, aimed at improving the classification of patients with kidney disease due to monoclonal proteins whose clinical course does not meet the criteria for a hematologic cancer. The establishment of this diagnosis has been instrumental in tailoring specific and efficient therapy for such patients. Whereas the new term was created to highlight the premalignant nature of the monoclonal gammopathies, it also differentiates patients who have a monoclonal immunoglobulin–related kidney disease from those with MGUS. Clone-directed therapy is justified in the treatment of MGRS and does not violate the current diagnostic criteria and treatment recommendations for MGUS. With the addition of MGRS as a category of kidney diseases, treatment is no longer directed by the histopathological findings alone or by the tumor burden. Indeed, clone-directed therapy has been shown to preserve kidney function and prevent and treat recurrent disease after kidney transplantation in instances in which immunosuppression has been ineffective. The recognition that premalignant conditions can be responsible for end-organ damage has spawned the concept of monoclonal gammopathy of clinical significance (MGCS).[**66**](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMra1810907#core-r66) The concepts of both MGRS and MGCS are improving our understanding of the pathogenic manifestations of monoclonal proteins and, one hopes, may lead to better treatments with even fewer adverse effects.
