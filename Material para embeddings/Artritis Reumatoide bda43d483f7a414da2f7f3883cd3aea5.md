# Artritis Reumatoide

Enfermedad crónica, autoinmune e inflamatoria que afecta principalmente las articulaciones diartrodiales y cuya etiología es desconocida. Es una enfermedad sistémica.

### **Epidemiología**

- Incidencia anual: 40 por 100.000 habitantes
- Prevalencia: 0,4 a 1%
- Relación mujeres: hombres >3:1, pero a edades más avanzadas se equiparan.
- Edad: mujeres es a menor edad 45 años promedio y hombres 50 años promedio. Aumenta con la edad y comienza a disminuir en la séptima década de la vida. ***Late-onset RA (LORA) se presenta después de 60 a 65 años***
- La incidencia en poblaciones europeas y norteamericanas varía según la población:
- Norteamérica y Europa del norte tienen las prevalencias más altas: 0,4 a 1%
    - Lo más alto en EEUU fue evaluado en el Rochester Epidemiology Proyect (Mayo clinic y hospitales asociados): Incidencia anual 44.6/100.000 (mujeres 57, hombres 30)
- Menor en Francia (8.8/100.000)
- Mayor en países escandinavos (24-36/100.000)
- Europa del sur presenta prevalencias bajas 0,3 a 0,7%
- África y Asia con las prevalencias más bajas 0,2 a 0,3%
- Aumentan en áreas urbanas

Históricamente las más altas prevalencias se han registrado en Indios Inupiat, Chippewa y Pima (5%). También se vio una mayor tasa de prevalencia en Mexicanos que viven en US respecto a blancos y negros. Se han descrito hallazgos de lesiones reumatoides en esqueletos de nativos americanos los que no se encuentran en ancestros europeos.

***Factores sociales***: De acuerdo a estudio GLADAR 77% tienen menos de 12 años de educación y 60% son de grupo socioeconómico medio a bajo.

**En Chile**

Existe un estudio de epidemiológico (Depto de Salud Pública de Escuela de Medicina UC, 2007) describe una prevalencia de 0.46%, 90.000 pacientes con AR

Entre los 25 primeros problemas de salud en mujeres como causa de AVISA (años de vida ajustados por discapacidad) en el tramo de edad entre los 45 y 74 años.

Población mapuche sería más severa y estaría dado por el estatus socioeconómico (estudio de Riedemann P, Maluje V (1993) Epidemiología de las Enfermedades Reumatológicas en Chile)

***Impacto de enfermedad***

Aunque el curso de la enfermedad es heterogénea y variable dentro de los dos primeros años desde el diagnóstico los pacientes suelen experimentar discapacidad moderada, y después de 10 años el 30% son discapacitados severamente y aproximadamente un tercio de los pacientes deja de trabajar en los dos años siguientes del inicio de la enfermedad.

- 5 a 20% son autovalentes
- 10% incapacidad laboral al año, 50% de los pactes van a ser incapaces de trabajar a los diez años del dg.
- HAQ >2  90% no trabajan

Tienen peor calidad de vida frente a la población general según cuestionarios EQ5D, SF36 y HAQ.

Altos gastos por hospitalizaciones además de fármacos: 1/3 fármacos y 1/3 pérdida utilidad y el resto en  atención salud y familia.

***Mortalidad***

Expectativa de vida menor: En promedio 7 años, lo mismo pasa en Chile.

Tasa mortalidad estandarizada 2 veces la población general.

La AR en si es una causa de mortalidad directa poco frecuente (aprox un 25%) dada por: erosión atlantoaxoidea, fibrosis pulmonar, Felty con pancitopenia, vasculitis y amiloidosis.

La ***principal causa de muerte es CV*** más riesgo Aterosclerosis (43%)

- Tienen mayor riesgo de Linfoma 2.7 veces vs población general.

En relación al tratamiento, MTX y anti TNF en AR (por sí solos) se ha asociado a disminución de mortalidad CV

Los ***predictores de mortalidad precoz*** por AR son: VHS alta, FR (+) y numero articulaciones inflamadas y que no responda a DMARDs (Rev sist Annals, 2009)

En cuanto a la serología (BMJ 2015):

- FR y anti CCP (+)  aumento de mortalidad global (HR 1.47)
- Anti CCP (+)  mayor mortalidad CV (HR 1.5)
- FR (+)  mayor mortalidad respiratoria y por neoplasias (HR 1.7 y 1.6 respect)

***Predictores de mortalidad en AR:***

- Nivel socioeconómico
- Hábitos
- Genético
- Actividad basal y acumulada
- Discapacidad
- FR
- comorbilidades

## **Patogenia en Artritis Reumatoide**

Desorden crónico inflamatorio que afecta típicamente a articulaciones pequeñas y medianas en forma simétrica.

- Lesión primaria es **sinovitis** donde **células inmunes invaden el sinovio** que es relativamente acelular lo que lleva al “pannus” inflamatorio.
    - El Pannus causa daño cartilaginoso, erosión ósea y finalmente pérdida de función de la articulación afectada.
- Compromiso sistémico pulmonar, cardiovascular y hematológico también puede pasar.
- Aumento de riesgo de aterosclerosis y linfoma.
- Si no es controlada se asocia a baja de expectativa de vida.

### **Factores genéticos**

Contribuyen un 50% del desarrollo de la enfermedad, factor de riesgo predominante. Concordancia 15 a 30% en monocigotos y 5% en dicigotos, comparados a 1% de la población. Herencia de AR un 60 a 65%.

Hay más de 100 loci asociados a factor de riesgo de desarrollar la enfermedad

    - Locus que codifica **HLA clase II en cromosoma 6p21.3**
    - Se asocia a severidad de AR y CCP (+)

### **Factores ambientales**

**Hormonales**

- Más frecuente en mujeres
- Efecto estrogénico sobre el sistema inmune
    - Inhibe función de T supresoras y mejora función de T helper
    - Presenta receptores de estrógenos en las células sinoviales y T de memoria
- Nuliparidad se asocia a más riesgo
- Hombres con AR tienen testosterona más baja y niveles altos de estrógenos
- Niveles más bajos de DHEA (hormona androgénica deshidroepiandrosterona)
- Menarquia más tardía tienen menos riesgo
- El embarazo es factor de riesgo, se ha visto que 12% parten 1 años después del embarazo, aunque en el embarazo baja la actividad, con recurrencia 3 meses postparto. Multiparidad se asocia a un curso más severo.

**Tabaco**

- más riesgo si hay epítope compartido, hasta 20 veces
- Específicamente el tiempo de fumar, no tanto la cantidad
- Factor de riesgo para enfermedad severa
- Enfermedad más severa, con FR (+), más nódulos y erosiones

Otros

- Obesidad (OR 3,15)
- Consumo de café, pero no ha sido confirmado
- Exposición ocupacional a aceites minerales (motor, hidraulico)
- Enfermedad periodontal por *Porphyromonas gingivalis* y *Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans* que generan PAD y enolasa (hay que prevenir periodontitis) - Se asocia a anti CCP
- Polvo sílice, asbesto, fibra de vidrio
- Embarazo
- Déficit de vitamina D
- Microbiota: menos diversidad en AR, aumento de Actinobacterias y de Prevotella copri, menos Bacteroides, Bifidobacterium. Pero los probióticos no han mostrado efecto.
- Infecciones víricas: PVB19, VEB. Rubeola, CMV
- Protectores: alcohol, inicio de menarquia antes de los 12 años, dieta mediterranea, acidos grasos omega 3

**Modificaciones epigenéticas**

- Alteran la transcripción de citoquinas y es diferente que en otras enfermedades
- Asociada a Tabaquismo

### **Autoanticuerpos**

**Factor reumatoideo:**

- Autoanticuerpos **contra porción Fc de la IgG humana**, principalmente IgM, pero también en subgrupos IgG e IgA
    - También pueden existir contra Fab de IgG humana, rol no claro
    - Habitualmente se mide IgM
        - IgG se ha asociado a vasculitis
        - IgA se ha visto más asociado a pérdida de cartílago y erosiones
- Forman **complejos inmunes** que activan complemento en las articulaciones que aumenta la permeabilidad vascular y liberación factores quimiotácticos que reclutan efectores inmunes en la articulación
- **Insuficiente para generar AR**, pueden ser encontradas en otras causas como infecciosas, autoinmunes, personas sanas 15%, especialmente en adultos mayores
- Sensibilidad es limitada (70% aprox), algunos plantean subir el corte a 50 UI

**Anticuerpos anti péptido citrulinado**

    - **Arginina (cargada +) -** >  **citrulina (**neutra)
        - Sale H, entra O
        - Sale un grupo amino
        - Cambia conformación tridimensional de proteinas

Moreover, one SNP located on the *OPG (osteoprogenerin)* gene has been associated with erosive arthritis (Ruyssen-Witrand A et al, 2016).

A recent study of patients with established RA, whose multiple serum samples have been analysed for the presence of elevated  levels of autoantibodies prior to diagnosis of RA, has found that both ACPA and RF were increased in the serum prior to diagnosis. ACPA-IgG was found to be elevated earlier compared to other autoantibody isotypes (17 years prior to diagnosis), and there was an initial early elevation of autoantibody levels, followed by a period of stability, then an increase in levels immediately prior to diagnosis. The early increase in serum autoantibody levels preceding RA in this cohort was associated with increased use of biologic therapy, and a higher prevalence of sicca symptoms and lung disease following RA diagnosis (Kelmenson et al, 2020).

    ***Acción celular:***
    
    *Las células dendríticas activan a los LT en los Linfonodos. Los LT son activados y ayudan a los LB en la producción de autoAc, los que forman inmunocomplejos que son acumulados en las articulaciones activando complemento. Esta activación del complemento recluta efectores celulares (macrófagos, neutrófilos, etc) que secretan citoquinas proinflamatorias y activan a los osteoclastos (proceso de destrucción del tejido articular).*
    
    *CD4+ que genera respuesta Th1 (producción de interferón gamma), Th2 (IL-4/5/13) y Th17 (IL-17/22 y GM-CSF) con principal objetivo en el control de infecciones (efectores en macrófagos, eosinófilos y neutrófilos). IL-12 induce respuesta Th1, IL-4 respuesta Th2 y TGF-B + IL-6 inducen Th17 (IL-1B y IL-23 tienen importante rol en inducir respuesta Th17).*
    
    *IL-17 tiene rol pleotrópico, con muchas acciones diferentes (angiogénesis, osteoclastogénesis y destrucción ósea).*
    
    *Treg (también derivadas desde los CD4+) juegan un rol crítico en suprimir respuesta inmune y de la tolerancia autoinmune (alteración en acción de Foxp3).*
    
    *Coestimulación en la activación de los LT en la presencia de receptores de superficie CD28 y de CD80/86 (inhibición con acción del Abatacept) conocida como el “inmune checkpoint”*
    
    *LB como productores de AutoAc y como célula presentadora de antígenos (tratamiento con RTX) con invasión de la articulación de estos mediante estímulo de factores como BAFF, CXCL12/13, APRIL.*
    
    *Daño articular mediado por macrófagos es un daño directo, secreción de mediadores responsables de la activación del complemento, TLR, secreta TNFa, IL-1, IL6, MMP9/12, GM-CSF (blanco de GC, MTX, LFN, biológicos como el IFX/RTX).*
    
    *En el daño articular se pueden generar **3 patrones histológicos en la AR:** 1) infiltrado inflamatorio de linfocitos y macrófagos (el más común), 2) presencia de folículos linfoides y 3) presencia de granulomas similares a los nódulos reumatoides (mas raro). Se genera hiperplasia del revestimiento articular, Neoangiogénesis (tercer marcador histológico en la sinovitis de la AR).*
    
    *Degradación articular y del hueso: Invasión de tejido inflamatorio del cartílago adyacente y del hueso subcondral es lo que se le llama pannus (infiltración y  proliferación sinovial). Fibroblastos sinoviales se adhieren al cartílago y degradan la matriz extracelular (se genera una hiperplasia sinovial por la mayor migración de los fibroblastos a la sinovial en la AR); los osteoclastos generan el daño óseo (ambas células interactúan con las células del sistema inmune que se encuentran activadas). Pro-osteoclasto secreta RANK (y también de LT) activándose la osteoclastogénesis (TNFa acelera este proceso).*

## **Manifestaciones clínicas**

- Enfermedad inflamatoria articular más prevalente. Peak de incidencia en mujeres de 50 a 60 años.
- Es una condición heterogénea
- **Inicio de la enfermedad**
    - Manifestaciones articulares
        - Típica: IFP, MCF, muñeca, MTTF
        - Puede empezar gradualmente hasta la poliartritis clásicamente simétrica. Puede manifestarse como monoartritis de rodilla ocasionalmente.
        - **IFD es rara**, menos de 10% de los casos
        - En pacientes ancianos puede iniciarse cercano a hombros y caderas.
    - Manifestaciones extraarticulares
        - Fiebre, compromiso orgánico (pericarditis o pleuritis)
        - Síndrome sicca, vasculitis, nódulos, dilatación bronquial o EPID
    - Síntomas
        - Dolor articular e hinchazón
        - Rigidez > 30 min
        - Fatiga
    - Examen físico
        - Edema de partes blandas y sensibilidad de las articulaciones
        - Limitación del rango de movimiento
        - “Squeeze test”
            
            El problema es que está más asociado a articulaciones doloras que inflamadas. Tiene una baja sensibilidad y moderada especificidad para artritis.
            
        - Examen general, buscando serositis, EPID. Esplenomegalia (Felty)
- **Enfermedad establecida**
    - Deformaciones en manos y muñecas
        - Boutonniere, flexión IFP, hiperextensión IFD, por relajación o desplazamiento volar del tendón central IFP.
        - Deformación en Z del pulgar por flexión de  MCF e hiperextensión IFD en 90°
        - Deformación en cuello de cisne por hiperextensión IFP y flexión IFD
        - Subluxación volar
        - Desviación ulnar
        - Tecla de piano en subluxación dorsal de radioulnar distal
    - Codo
        - Codo es más frecuente el daño por AR que por OA. Se ve ***pérdida de la extensión con una flexión fija. Se pierde supinación y flexión.***
    - Hombros
        - Menos común, se ve más en pacientes con AR severa.
        - Sinovitis produce erosión y daño en cabeza humeral y fosa glenoidea. La cabeza larga del biceps se puede romper. Se inflama el manguito rotador.
    - Columna cervical
        - Raro, pero puede ser grave.
        - Ligamento transverso del atlas posterior a odontoides tiene una articulación sinovial. La sinovitis a esta altura puede producir erosiones del diente y resultar en inestabilidad.
        - El espacio entre odontoides anterior y superficie posterior del arco anterior del atlas normalmente tiene menos de 3 mm, si excede los **5 mm**, se considera subluxación atlantoaxoidal.
        - Con más de **10 mm** suele existir mielopatía cervical.
        - Fatal sería cuando dislocación es vertical hasta foramen magno (invaginación basilar)
        - En pacientes de este tipo para evitar hiperextender, se prefiere guiados por fibrobroncoscopía. Antes de operar se pide radiografía con posición neutral, flexión o buscando el odontoides con la boca abierta.

<aside>
👉🏽

Reumatismo palindrómico: episodios variables de poliartritis, que afectan una o más articulaciones grandes y pequeñas, desde unas pocas hora a días, seguido de resolución espontánea. 1/3 desarrolla AR.

</aside>

*AR precoz particular el reumatismo palindrómico (AR por crisis episódicas de duración de algunas horas o días, manteniéndose asintomático entre episodios).*

*Lo común es que se inicie con compromiso articular progresivo llegando a ser poliarticular y simétrico (raro como monoartritis, por ej en rodilla).*

*Otra forma de clasificación clínica es por los patrones de inicio de la enfermedad: Patrón lento e insidioso (50-70%) en donde la inflamación articular es aditiva, patrón agudo (8-15%) o patrón intermedio (15-20%).*

*Según el curso evolutivo puede ser de curso intermitente (15-20%) con periodos de remisión parcial o total, curso remitente (10%) con periodos de remisión prolongados y curso progresivo (65-70%) que es el más frecuente con afectación aditiva de articulaciones.*

*La evolución de la enfermedad es a la pérdida laboral en un 50% de los pactes a 10 años con un aumento de la mortalidad entre 3 a 18 años (enfermedades cardiovasculares, infecciones, enfermedades renales y enfermedades pulmonares).*

*El pannus es un infiltrado inflamatorio consistente de células mononucleares (LT CD4+, macrófagos activados, LB, células plasmáticas algunas de las cuales generan FR y ACPA, células dendríticas que organizan la estructura como si fuera un linfonodo), las que estimuladas por el ambiente de citoquinas proliferan en la sinovial (generando proyecciones vellosas de la sinovial) siendo capaz de invadir el hueso y cartílago (destruyendo la articulación).*

*Las deformidades articulares presentes son la deformidad en Boutonnière (flexión de la IFP y hiperextensión de la IFD causada por la debilidad del tendón del extensor), cuello de cisne (como resultado de la contracción de los flexores de la MCF con hiperextensión de la IFP y flexión de la IFD), desviación ulnar de los dedos (sublunxación de las MCF por debilidad del extensor ulnar del carpo), pulgar de Hitchhiker (hiperextensión de IF con flexión de la MCF con abducción exagerada del 1era Metacarpo generando una deformidad en “Z”), tecla de piano de la cabeza ulnar (por la destrucción del ligamento colateral ulnar generando un estiloides ulnar flotante).*

*El compromiso del pie puede verse en un 33% de los casos de AR, lo que puede ser la primera manifestación. Se manifiesta la dificultad de la pesquisa de esta sinovitis, planteando el squeeze test como una alternativa de pesquisa en estos casos. La manifestación mas común son los dedos en garra o los dedos en martillo como secundario a la inflamación de las MTF con subluxación de la cabeza de los MTT (lo que se manifiesta como alteraciones en los puntos de apoyo con callosidades).*

*El compromiso cervical se encontraba entre un 30-50% de los pctes con AR (C1-C2) lo que ha ido disminuyendo el tiempo por la mejora de las terapias. Puede ocurrir en paralelo con la progresión de la artritis periférica. La clínica mas común es el dolor con irradiación al occipucio (subluxación), irradiación nerviosa y muerte. Puede ocurrir una subluxación C1-C2 (65%) anterior (>3 mms), lateral y posterior atlanto-axoidea. Esto ocurre por la proliferación de la sinovial alrededor del proceso odontoide con el arco anterior de C1 generando daño o rotura del ligamento transverso. Otro compromiso es la impactación C1-C2 (25%) que puede ser la migración del proceso odontoide, lo que es secundario a la destrucción articular con movilización del odontoide al foramen magno. El compromiso Subaxial (15%) incluye C2-C3 y C3-C4 en sus fascetas y los discos inter-vertebrales con sub-luxación entre las vertebra.*

*El estudio del líquido sinovial en la AR es de un líquido inflamatorio con GB típicamente entre 5.000-50.000/mm3 sin signos de infección, con predominio de PMN (>50%), nivel de proteínas elevado y niveles de glucosa puede estar bajo los valores séricos (40-60% de los valores séricos), sin cristales.*

*Dentro de las manifestaciones extra-articulares, la presencia de fiebre es rara, por lo que se debe descartar otras causas de esta (infección en primera instancia, procesos linfoproliferativos).*

*El nódulo reumatoide son subcutáneos con histología de un área central de necrosis rodeada de zonas de elongaciones histocitarias y un anillo periférico de tejido conectivo. Ocurre entre un **20-35% (compromiso extrarticular mas frecuente)** de los pacientes y son AR con FR (+) y una enfermedad severa. Se encuentran en las áreas extensoras (bursa olecraneana y otras zonas de presión) y se correlaciona con la actividad de la enfermedad. El MTX puede generar nodulosis (nódulos más pequeños) en un pequeño grupo de pacientes. Granuloma anular es el diagnóstico diferencial (en pcte con FR (-) y en  niños).*

***AR del adulto mayor:***

*Se define como aquella AR de inicio sobre los 60-65 años. La presentación clínica presenta ciertas diferencias, por ejemplo que la distribución es similar entre hombres y mujeres, es de inicio súbito o brusco como poliartralgia y polimialgia con compromiso principal de hombros y cuello (o cadera y rodillas) con mas rigidez matinal, mas fatiga  y puede tener fiebre. Se le asocia a elevación de parámetros inflamatorios al debut, FR y ACPA negativos y daño articular precoz.*

*Dentro de la terapia, se tiende a utilizar menor MTX y biológicos, con una mayor tendencia al uso de GC.*

*El debut de AR en el adulto mayor se considera como un factor de mal pronóstico ya que presenta mayor riesgo cardiovascular, de neoplasia y de posibilidad de desarrollar infecciones.*

    Felty’s syndrome and T-LGL leukemia—a common disease
    spectrum?
    As explained in detail later, T-LGL leukemia is
    characterized by monoclonal expansion of T-LGLs (mostly CD8+), which usually comprise only
    10–15% of mononuclear blood cells in healthy individuals (1). Interestingly, expansion of
    T-LGLs is present in many patients with Felty’s syndrome as well, although mostly poly−/
    
    oligoclonal. In one study comprising 21 patients with T-LGL lymphocytosis, 9 patients (=43%) suffered from seropositive rheumatoid arthritis, splenomegaly and neutropenia and were therefore regarded as having Felty’s syndrome. After assessing clonality in those patients using T-cell receptor studies, 3 patients fulfilled the WHO (world health organization) criteria for T-LGL leukemia. Another 3 patients also had monoclonal T-LGLs, but T-LGL count was <2 × 109/L and 2 patients had polyclonal expansion of T-LGLs, which is a common finding in autoimmune diseases. Interestingly, there was a mixed T-LGL composition in 1 patient, showing polyclonal as well as monoclonal T-LGLs simultaneously, which was interpreted as a possible transition state (2). But whether the T-LGL lymphocytosis that accompanies some cases of Felty’s syndrome can degenerate into T-LGL leukemia is unknown so far. Consecutive clonality studies in patients with Felty’s syndrome might be useful to examine if such a transition is possible and whether it can be influenced by immunosuppressive treatment.
    A major driver of clonal T-LGL expansion in T-LGL leukemia is dysregulated JAK/STAT signaling, caused by somatic gain-of-function mutations in STAT3 (signal transducer and activator of transcription 3). STAT3 acts as a transcription factor with anti-apoptotic as well as proliferative effects and additionally, its activation in T-LGL leukemia patients correlates with FasL (Fas ligand) levels, explaining the occurrence of neutropenia in these patients (3). STAT3 mutations were noted in 30–70% of T-LGL leukemia cases and although not pathognomonic for this disease, the detection of STAT3 mutations can aid in T-LGL leukemia diagnosis in an appropriate clinical context. Savola et al. reported STAT3 mutations in 40% of FS patients as well (4), leading to speculations about a common pathogenesis of T-LGL leukemia and Felty’s syndrome. However, a recent study found STAT3 mutations in only 10% of FS cases (5). In this study, T-LGL clonality was more thoroughly assessed to allow a better distinction between FS cases and patients with T-LGL leukemia. The FS population studied by Savola et al. was heterogenous though, possibly leading to a higher STAT3 mutation detection rate because of admixed T-LGL leukemia cases.
    Another similarity of Felty’s syndrome and T-LGL leukemia was suggested by older studies showing a frequent expression of HLA-DRB1 alleles, especially HLA-DRB1*04:01 and HLA-DRB1*04:04 (HLA = human leukocyte antigen). HLA-DRB1 was expressed in 90% of cases with Felty’s syndrome as well as in patients with T-LGL leukemia in the wake of rheumatoid arthritis. By contrast, there was no significant HLA-association in patients with T-LGL leukemia without accompanying rheumatoid arthritis (6). However, as the studied FS populations at that time likely contained T-LGL leukemia patients as well due to insufficient clonality assessments, those findings should be interpreted with caution.
    Taken together, it is yet unclear whether Felty’s syndrome and T-LGL leukemia form a common disease spectrum and many studies were based on heterogenous populations, hampering interpretation.
    Possible role of NETs in autoimmunity and neutropenia
    The secretion of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) is another defense mechanism of neutrophils alongside phagocytosis and degranulation of antimicrobial granules. NETs have recently attracted attention in autoimmunity research and they might play an important role in the pathogenesis of Felty’s syndrome as well. The exact mechanisms of NET formation are still unknown, but a variety of pathogens (bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa), cytokines [e.g., tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα), interleukin 8 (IL-8), interferon α (IFNα)] and chemical noxae (e.g., hydrogen peroxide, nitric oxide) are able to trigger NET formation in neutrophils. During this process, enzymes like MPO (myeloperoxidase) and NE (neutrophil elastase) translocate into the nucleus and break down histones, leading to chromatin decondensation. The chromatin then passes the damaged nuclear membrane and enters the cytoplasm, where it encounters various proteins. A web consisting of DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), antimicrobial enzymes (e.g., proteinase 3, cathepsin G, myeloperoxidase) and other antimicrobial agents (e.g., lactoferrin, cathelicidin) is finally released into the extracellular space. The negatively charged DNA and the various positively charged proteins attached upon it allow for numerous non-covalent interactions with the surface of pathogens, which are literally trapped and killed in those nets. It is thought that NET formation comes at the expense of neutrophil death and this process was termed “NETosis “by analogy with other forms of cell death. But subsequently, NET formation was detected in living, albeit anuclear neutrophils as well (7), which might then be removed by macrophages. Overall, NET formation thus comes along with neutrophil consumption.
    Additionally to their antimicrobial effects, NETs might play an important role in the development of autoimmune diseases too, as their secretion into the extracellular space expose various intracellular antigens, some of which are well known in different diseases (e.g., DNA, MPO, proteinase 3) (8). The pathogenetic importance of NETs is best known in SLE (systemic lupus erythematosus), wherein NETs not only represent a source of antigens but also have cytotoxic effects on endothelial cells and therefore broader pathogenetic implications.
    During NET formation, histones can undergo post-translational modifications (e.g., acetylation, methylation, citrullination) (9) and ACPAs (anti-citrullinated protein antibodies) in RA patients reacted with citrullinated and even with acetylated histones in NETs (10). Similarly, patients with Felty’s syndrome had antibodies against NET components including citrullinated histones (11). Therefore, it is tempting to postulate a pathogenetic role of NET formation and NETosis in Felty’s syndrome and FS-associated neutropenia. However, additional pathophysiological studies are necessary in this regard and as only a minor subset of RA patients develops Felty’s syndrome, the presumptive role of NETs in Felty’s syndrome pathogenesis can only be a partial one.
    Other causes of neutropenia
    The emergence of neutropenia in Felty’s syndrome seems to be multifactorial and supposed mechanisms include increased peripheral destruction as well as suppressed production in the bone marrow. Antibodies against neutrophil antigens that lead to complement activation, opsonization and consecutive phagocytosis by macrophages in the spleen and bone marrow is a well-accepted mechanism causing primary autoimmune neutropenia (AIN). In AIN, the autoantibodies act against surface proteins like Fc-gamma receptor IIIb or CD11b (an integrin) on neutrophils (12). However, much less is known about the neutrophil antigens responsible for

    neutropenia in Felty’s syndrome. Ditzel et al. identified autoantibodies against eukaryotic elongation factor 1A-1 (eEF1A-1) (13), but whether these antibodies cause neutropenia is unknown to date. Van Gaalen et al. postulated, that antibodies against glucose-6-phosphate isomerase (G6PI-Ab) might contribute to neutropenia via forming of G6PI-Ab containing immune complexes on vessel walls, leading to margination of neutrophils (14). However, as later studies detected G6PI-Ab not only in FS patients, but also in other rheumatic diseases that are not associated with neutropenia (e.g., gout, spondyloarthritis) (15), the role of G6PI-Ab in Felty’s syndrome remains elusive.
    Another cause of neutropenia in Felty’s syndrome might be Fas-mediated apoptosis. Fas receptor (Fas, CD95) and Fas ligand (FasL, CD95L) both exist as membrane-bound (mFas and mFasL respectively) as well as soluble forms (sFas and sFasL respectively). Interaction between Fas and FasL can have different impacts on the cells involved, from which apoptosis of the Fas-expressing cell is best known. FasL is expressed by cytotoxic T-cells, which are thus able to kill Fas-expressing cells (e.g., antigen-presenting cells like dendritic cells or neutrophils). Because T-cells express both Fas and FasL following their activation, the Fas/FasL pathway is also important for preventing an overshooting T-cell response. This process called activation-induced cell death (AICP) might play a crucial role in immune regulation and autoimmunity prevention.
    Studies with patients suffering from rheumatoid arthritis or T-LGL leukemia found elevated sFasL serum levels (16, 17) and at least in T-LGL leukemia patients, sFasL-mediated apoptosis of neutrophils was demonstrated in vitro (16). Interestingly, the Fas/FasL pathway can also have an anti-apoptotic effect. In T-LGL leukemia patients, Fas/FasL-mediated apoptosis of leukemic cells was blocked. This might be due to high serum levels of sFas found in this study, which occupies FasL and thus prevents its interaction with Fas, rendering leukemic cells resistant to apoptosis (18).
    Additionally, antibodies against G-CSF (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor) might play a role in neutropenia. A study with 15 Felty’s syndrome patients detected anti-G-CSF IgG antibodies in 11 cases. At least in 3 from 9 anti-G-CSF positive cases (=33%), these antibodies had a neutralizing effect on G-CSF too. But because G-CSF activity was unaffected in the remaining study participants, additional mechanisms like a reduced responsivity of neutrophils to G-CSF was suggested. Interestingly, anti-neutrophil antibodies were detected as well in all 7 patients tested who were anti-G-CSF positive (19).
    In 1983, bone marrow-directed mechanisms in 27 FS patients were investigated in one study. Cell cultures showed less colony forming units (CFU) compared to controls. In 7 patients, bone marrow suppression was attributed to CD8+ T-cells and in 5 patients, an unknown serum inhibitory factor led to decreased CFUs. Feeder layers from another 5 patients failed to induce CFUs in control’s bone marrow cultures, which was suggested to be caused by a lack of colony stimulating factor (CSF) generation in FS patients (20). Again, it should be recognized that these results might be confounded by a heterogeneous study population (i.e., possibly admixed T-LGL leukemia cases) and newer studies would be useful as a proof. Whether the CD8+ T-cells in the above study represented T-LGLs is unknown, but T-LGLs were shown to suppress granulocyte-macrophage progenitor cells in vitro (21) although there is still uncertainty about how this suppression is mediated (i.e., via cell–cell interaction or via cytokines).
    Causes of pancytopenia in Felty’s syndrome
    There are various presumed pathomechanisms for neutropenia (i.e., increased neutrophil consumption in the context of NETosis, Fas-mediated apoptosis, suppression of granulopoiesis by T-LGLs, anti-neutrophil antibodies, anti-G-CSF antibodies), but Felty’s syndrome as well as T-LGL leukemia can sometimes present with pancytopenia, whose causes are less well understood. One possible explanation for pancytopenia is an increased sequestration of blood cells in the spleen due to splenomegaly. Histopathological studies examining spleens from patients with Felty’s syndrome identified unspecific changes like an enlarged red pulp, plasmacytosis and hyperplastic lymph follicles as the histological correlate of splenomegaly (22). In T-LGL leukemia, the red pulp is expanded as well, but additionally the spleen is infiltrated by monoclonal T-cells (23). Strikingly, splenic size does not correlate with the extent of cytopenia (24, 25) and splenectomy was found to only partially or transiently revert cytopenia (24, 25). These findings suggest that hypersplenism cannot be the sole reason for pancytopenia. It is likely that T-LGLs account for anemia and thrombopenia in a similar way as they do in neutropenia. In the 1990s, T-LGLs were found to be able to lyse erythroblasts via interaction between MHC I (major histocompatibility complex) molecules and KIR receptors (killer cell inhibitory receptor) (26). KIR receptors are actually a characteristic feature of natural killer (NK) cells but are expressed on T-LGLs as well. They interact with MHC I molecules (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C) and can be divided into activating and inhibitory KIR receptors according to their molecular structure. Overall, KIR receptors facilitate the detection of abnormal cells like infected or neoplastic cells, which exhibit an aberrant HLA expression (i.e., “missing self “hypothesis). It was found that each KIR receptor has a distinct HLA ligand (e.g., the ligand for KIR 2DL1 is HLA-C2), which is called a “KIR/HLA match” (27). Nowakowski et al. studied the KIR receptor phenotype as well as the HLA I genotype in 7 patients with T-LGL leukemia. In 5 patients, there was a KIR/HLA mismatch, which means that the HLA I molecule could not be identified by the KIR receptor. All these patients had pancytopenia, whereas the remaining patients with KIR/HLA match had a normal blood count (28). Because clonal T-LGLs usually express just one KIR isoform (or rarely two), it is conceivable that the resulting KIR/HLA mismatch leads to lysis of hematopoietic progenitor cells and pancytopenia. Even T-CUS (T-cell clonopathy of unknown significance) as well as polyclonal T-LGL expansion were linked with the suppression of hematopoiesis (29, 30). As mentioned earlier, Felty’s syndrome is often associated with T-LGL expansion, suggesting its role in cytopenia. However, causation still needs to be proven through studies involving patients with Felty’s syndrome.
    Neutrophil dysfunction
    Older studies conducted by Ruderman et al. and Sienknecht et al. already demonstrated that there is no correlation between the extent of leucopenia and the number of infections in patients with Felty’s syndrome (24, 25). Strikingly, even raising neutrophil counts by splenectomy did not alter infection rate significantly (24), suggesting the contribution of neutrophil dysfunction. Subsequently, studies found an impaired phagocytosis of opsonized bacteria, which was attributed to a diminished expression of Fc receptors on neutrophils. This reduction of Fc receptors is thought to be due to circulating immune complexes that bind to Fc receptors, causing their internalization (31).
    Studies conducted in the 1970s and 1980s also demonstrated impaired chemotaxis (32, 33) as well as decreased superoxide production (34). Neither of these findings was examined in more recent studies.
    
    Clinical manifestations
    Rheumatoid arthritis in Felty’s syndrome is usually seropositive and severe, leading to erosions and joint deformities. This had been revealed in older studies as well as in a more recent cohort study, which found joint erosions in 77% of Felty’s syndrome patients at the time of diagnosis (35). In this cohort study, 92 and 96% of cases tested positive for rheumatoid factor and ACPA, respectively (35). Different studies also demonstrated ANA (antinuclear antibodies) positivity in 55–84% of cases (24, 25, 36, 37) and in 6% of patients, antibodies against double-stranded DNA were detected (36). Usually, joint disease precedes the emergence of neutropenia and splenomegaly by several years (11–16 years on average) (36, 37). Only in isolated cases, neutropenia and splenomegaly are diagnosed before or at the onset of rheumatoid arthritis (35, 38). When compared to rheumatoid arthritis without accompanying Felty’s-Syndrome, Felty’s syndrome is more often paralleled by extraarticular manifestations of RA, like rheumatoid nodules (71–82% of cases), vasculitis (24–28%), pleuritis (15–22%), episcleritis (3–11%), and pericarditis (7%). Skin ulcers, mostly at the legs, are noted in 78% of cases (24, 36, 37). These ulcers have a multifactorial etiology, including common causes like chronic venous insufficiency and atherosclerosis as well as vasculitis and ulcerated rheumatoid nodules (39, 40). Skin ulcers often get contaminated and their occurrence correlates with infection risk (40). Other clinical manifestations of Felty’s syndrome include lymphadenopathy (16–42% of cases) and yellowish/brown skin pigmentations (24, 25, 36, 37).
    A prerequisite for diagnosis is neutropenia, defined as an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) below the reference range of the respective laboratory (in most laboratories, neutropenia is defined as ANC < 1,500/μL). About half of Felty’s syndrome patients present with severe neutropenia (ANC < 500/μL) and even agranulocytosis (ANC < 100/μL) is possible (35). Neutropenia increases the infection risk, with skin infections and respiratory infections being most frequent. Common Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria like Staphylococcus aureus, Haemophilus influenzae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Streptococcus spp. are involved. Left untreated, infection might proceed to sepsis and death, making the increase in neutrophil counts a main goal of therapy.
    Additionally to neutropenia, studies also reported varying rates of thrombopenia (15–48% of cases) and anemia (79–100%) (24, 25, 36). As mentioned above, hypersplenism and myelosuppression are possible reasons for pancytopenia. Anemia can also be caused by chronic inflammation/active rheumatoid arthritis or by blood loss due to bleeding esophageal varices.
    Nowadays, splenomegaly is no longer a prerequisite for the diagnosis of Felty’s syndrome, but most patients will show some degree of splenomegaly. Sienknecht et al. for example reported splenic weights up to 1,650 g (normal spleen weight: 150–200 g) (24).
    Like other rheumatic diseases, Felty’s syndrome can be accompanied by nodular regenerative hyperplasia (NRH) of the liver, leading to non-cirrhotic portal hypertension. NRH is characterized by multiple small (<3 mm) regenerative nodules and only minimal fibrosis, as opposed to liver cirrhosis. NRH is thought to be caused by immune-mediated damage of small portal vein endothelium with consecutive hypoperfusion and apoptosis of hepatocytes. During the regeneration process, growth factors cause hyperplasia of adjacent hepatocytes, resulting in multiple nodules (41). In contrast to liver cirrhosis, liver function is preserved, but portal hypertension and its consequences (i.e., ascites, splenomegaly, esophageal varices) can develop similarly in NRH. Besides NRH, splenomegaly can contribute to portal hypertension as well due to increased blood flow in the portal venous system. Taken together, portal hypertension in Felty’s syndrome can be of intra-and prehepatic origin. The most serious sequel of portal hypertension is bleeding from esophageal varices, which occurred in some patients with Felty’s syndrome (42) and which is why endoscopic screening for varices can be recommended.
    Like rheumatoid arthritis, Felty’s syndrome is associated with an increased risk of malignancy. While there is a 2.5-fold higher lymphoma risk in RA when compared to the general population (43), this risk is even higher (5-fold) in Felty’s syndrome, as shown in a Swedish registry study involving 952 patients with Felty’s syndrome (44). Generally, it seems that malignancy risk correlates with the extent of inflammatory (i.e., rheumatoid arthritis) activity. Baecklund et al. studied the incidence of lymphomas taking account of RA activity and detected a 8-fold and 70-fold higher incidence for “moderate “and “high “RA activity, respectively, compared to patients with “low“RA activity (45). It is believed that the chronic immunostimulation facilitates clonal selection and finally malignant transformation of lymphocytes. Felty’s syndrome often represents a severe and difficult-to-treat form of rheumatoid arthritis, by which its higher lymphoma incidence, when compared to isolated RA, might be explained. Additionally, RA patients have a 1.6-fold higher risk for lung cancer (43), which is at least partially attributed to chronic inflammation too. On the other hand, the risk for colorectal cancer, breast cancer, cervical cancer, prostate cancer as well as melanoma in RA patients does not seem to be significantly increased compared to the general population (43). This might be similar in patients with Felty’s syndrome.
    Epidemiology
    The prevalence of Felty’s syndrome declined in the last decades from 1.5% in 1985 to currently 0.5%. This might be partially due to new classification criteria for LGL leukemia established in 1985. Before then, some cases of LGL leukemia might have been classified as Felty’s syndrome. Additionally, improved treatment options for rheumatoid arthritis since 1985 (e.g., methotrexate) might also play a role (46, 47). Felty’s syndrome is usually diagnosed between the fourth and sixth decade of life and women are affected in 60–85% of cases (24, 25, 35–37). Family history for rheumatoid arthritis is positive in

    Differential diagnosis
    The differential diagnosis of Felty’s syndrome includes various conditions presenting with cytopenia and/or splenomegaly (Supplementary Tables S1, S2). Even its typical triad of rheumatoid arthritis, splenomegaly and neutropenia does not allow a straightforward diagnosis, as RA might just be a coincidence. Therefore, it is essential to rule out malignant diseases, which usually requires bone marrow biopsy. Some hematological conditions and diseases that resemble Felty’s syndrome might be a bit confusing (Supplementary Table S3) and are therefore highlighted in the upcoming section.
    T-LGL and NK-LGL disorders
    Large granular lymphocytes usually comprise 10–15% of peripheral blood mononuclear cells. Most of them are T-cells (T-LGL) and only 15% represent NK-cells (NK-LGL). Both subsets can undergo malignant transformation, causing T-LGL and aggressive NK-LGL leukemia, respectively. T-LGL leukemia is an indolent disease which only requires treatment in the case of symptomatic cytopenia (usually neutropenia). Aggressive NK-LGL leukemia represents the exact opposite, with a median survival of <2 months (52). Many cases of T-LGL leukemia emerge in the wake of autoimmune diseases, thereby suggesting chronic (auto)antigen stimulus with consecutive clonal LGL selection as the underlying pathomechanism. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that LGLs typically exhibit a memory phenotype, thus must have encountered an antigen. Up to 25% of T-LGL leukemia cases occur in patients suffering from rheumatoid arthritis (53) and T-LGL leukemia is thus an important differential diagnosis of Felty’s syndrome. In T-LGL leukemia, neutropenia is seen in up to 84% of cases and studies also reported significant rates of anemia (24–89%) and thrombopenia (19–36%) (54). In contrast to Felty’s syndrome, men and women are equally affected in T-LGL leukemia and splenomegaly only occurs in 19–50% of cases (54), whereas its absence in Felty’s syndrome is exceptional. Unlike one might suggest from the term “leukemia“, lymphocytosis (absolute lymphocyte count >4 × 109/μL) is present in only 50% of cases and normal lymphocyte counts therefore do not exclude T-LGL leukemia. B symptoms and lymphadenopathy occur infrequently in T-LGL leukemia.
    Using flow cytometry from peripheral blood or bone marrow specimen, LGLs can be quantified and up to 0.25 × 109/L cells are regarded normal in healthy individuals, although there is some disagreement concerning this upper limit of normal. The WHO defines T-LGL leukemia as a persistent (>6 months) increase in T-LGL counts, usually to 2–20 × 109/L, without a clearly identified cause (52). However, it is common practice to consider even T-LGL counts <2 × 109/L as sufficient for T-LGL leukemia diagnosis, if both monoclonality and an appropriate clinical picture (e.g., cytopenia, recurrent infections, splenomegaly, accompanying autoimmune disease) are present (55).
    On the other hand, monoclonal T-LGL counts <2 × 109/L in the absence of symptoms are insufficient for the diagnosis of T-LGL leukemia and this constellation is often called T-CUS. In a case series involving 30 patients with T-CUS, clinical course remained indolent without any intervention in 80% during the follow-up period of 9 years, but some T-CUS cases developed hemolytic anemia, immune thrombocytopenia or transformed into T-LGL leukemia necessitating treatment (29).
    Most T-LGLs express CD8 like cytotoxic T-cells. Additionally, these cells usually exhibit a phenotype akin to terminal effector memory T-cells (i.e., CD45RA+ CD62L−) and express certain NK-cell markers (CD57, CD16) as well as the α/β T-cell receptor (α/β TCR). Uncommon variants include CD4+ T-LGLs with α/β TCR and CD8+ or CD4−CD8-T-LGLs with γ/δ T-cell receptor.
    Using PCR (polymerase chain reaction)-based T-cell receptor gene rearrangement studies or flow cytometric TCR-phenotyping, T-LGLs can be assessed for clonality. If there are not enough T-LGLs in the peripheral blood for analysis, bone marrow biopsy must be performed to get more sample material. In doing so, bone marrow can be concurrently assessed for infiltration by leukemic cells, which is usually present in T-LGL leukemia (52).
    In contrast to T-LGL leukemia, aggressive NK-LGL leukemia can usually be distinguished from Felty’s syndrome by its clinical course alone. Delineation of NK-LGLs can be done by flow cytometry (most frequent phenotype: CD3−CD4−CD8−CD16+CD56+CD57−) (52). Another difference to T-LGL leukemia is the lack of associated autoimmune disorders in aggressive NK-LGL leukemia.
    Besides T-LGL leukemia and aggressive NK-LGL leukemia, there exists a third monoclonal LGL disorder termed CLPD-NK (chronic lymphoproliferative disorder of NK cells) by the WHO. CLPD-NK is characterized by persistently (>6 months) increased NK-LGL counts (usually >2 × 109/L) without a clearly identified cause and like T-LGL leukemia, it is a mostly indolent condition, which sets it apart from aggressive NK-LGL leukemia (52).
    Reactive LGL lymphocytosis
    Various autoimmune disorders (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, Felty’s syndrome, vasculitides, SLE), viral infections (e.g., EBV, CMV, HIV) and hematologic diseases (e.g., myelodysplastic syndromes, aplastic anemia, myeloproliferative neoplasms) can be paralleled by a clonal expansion of T-LGLs and/or NK-LGLs. LGL expansion was also observed following transplantation of solid organs or hematopoietic stem cells and in the context of immunomodulatory therapies (e.g., dasatinib, rituximab) (52, 56, 57). In the case of viral infections and autoimmune diseases, chronic antigen stimulation with subsequent clonal selection is thought to be the underlying pathomechanisms of LGL lymphocytosis. Contrary to this, in hematologic diseases like myelodysplastic syndromes or aplastic anemia, it is less clear if LGL lymphocytosis really represents a “reactive “phenomenon, or is actually a part of the underlying pathology (58).

    LGL lymphocytosis is usually comprised of poly-or oligoclonal LGLs, which sets it apart from monoclonal LGL leukemia. Unfortunately, clonality assessment cannot differentiate all cases of LGL lymphocytosis from LGL leukemia because some viral infections have been linked with monoclonal LGL expansion too (59, 60).     Hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma
    HSTL is a rare and aggressive extranodal T-cell lymphoma with a median survival of less than 2 years. Median age at diagnosis is 35 years and men are affected more frequently (9-fold) than women. Approximately 20% of HSTL cases occur in the wake of chronic immunosuppression or chronic antigen stimulation (52, 62). Thus, HSTL was observed in patients following organ transplantation or chemotherapy as well as in the context of immunosuppressive treatment of inflammatory bowel disease or rheumatic disorders. Especially the use of thiopurines (azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurin) over a course of >2 years was linked to the emergence of HSTL, but some cases were also noted following exclusive TNFα inhibitor exposure. However, it is unknown whether the occurrence of HSTL is caused by immunosuppressive agents or by the underlying autoimmune disorder/immune dysregulation itself. Similar to LGL leukemia, chronic (auto)antigen stimulation with subsequent clonal selection is thought to be underlying pathomechanism of HSTL (63). HSTL is characterized by the occurrence of mature T-cells, which infiltrate spleen, liver and bone marrow, leading to hepatosplenomegaly and (pan)cytopenia (64). At first glance, confusion with Felty’s syndrome is thus possible but differentiation is usually easily accomplished using flow cytometric immunophenotyping. HSTL typically features CD4/CD8 negative T-cells expressing the γ/δ T-cell receptor subtype, whereas T-LGL leukemia is usually characterized by CD8+ T-cells with α/β T-cell receptor. However, it should be kept in mind that akin to T-LGL leukemia, there exist HSTL variants, that express the α/β T-cell receptor (52).
    Hairy cell leukemia
    HCL is a rare B-cell neoplasm that predominantly affects middle-aged to elderly adults and has a male preponderance. HCL is thought to arise from an activated memory B-cell that has acquired an activating gene mutation in BRAF, which leads to constitutive activation of the RAF-MEK-ERK signaling pathway (MEK: mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase, ERK: extracellular signal-regulated kinase). This results in enhanced cell survival and proliferation and is also the presumed cause for the cytoplasmic projections that give those cells their “hairy” morphology. The tumor cells predominantly infiltrate bone marrow and spleen, causing pancytopenia and splenomegaly. Occasionally, HCL can lead to PAN-like vasculitis (PAN: Polyarteritis nodosa), rendering clinical differentiation from Felty’s syndrome especially difficult. HCL diagnosis is made by bone marrow biopsy showing a distinctive cytomorphology in conjunction with immunophenotyping (52).
    Treatment
    When interpretating older studies, it should always be kept in mind that the study populations at that time were likely heterogeneous, containing FS patients as well as patients with T-LGL leukemia, because flow cytometric immunophenotyping and clonality studies were not available. This might have influenced treatment outcomes. The main treatment goal in Felty’s syndrome is the improvement of neutropenia and infection rate. Initially, splenectomy was the only treatment option in the absence of efficient pharmacotherapy. However, splenectomy regularly results in only transient increases of neutrophils. A sustained normalization of neutrophil counts over a period exceeding 6 months can be achieved in approximately 80% of cases (65), but this percentage often declines steadily over time [e.g., one study reported normal neutrophil counts in 62% of patients after 5 years follow-up (36)]. Infection rate is often not controlled by splenectomy either and roughly one third of patients continue to suffer from recurrent infections (65). Interestingly, there is no correlation between infection rate and neutrophil count. Besides neutropenia, splenectomy usually improves anemia and thrombopenia as well, although these manifestations are seldom severe enough to be clinically relevant, let alone necessitating splenectomy. Apart from its hematological ramifications, splenectomy also has beneficial effects on the healing of leg ulcers (37). Overall, splenectomy has become less important over time and today, its indication is mainly limited to recurrent infections despite medical therapies.
    As the occurrence of circulating immune complexes in Felty’s syndrome had been recognized early and had been attributed pathogenetic importance, there were several attempts to remove those immune complexes using plasmapheresis. But studies showed conflicting results (66), which is why plasmapheresis currently has no role in the treatment of Felty’s syndrome. This is also true for intravenous immune globulin (IVIG) therapy, which failed to improve neutrophil counts significantly in most studies (67).
    During the last decades, various agents including lithium, ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone), parenteral gold and D-penicillamine were tried as conservative treatment in Felty’s syndrome, but neither of them prevailed because of either inefficacy or intractable adverse reactions. In 1980, methotrexate was used in patients with Felty’s syndrome for the first time and it still represents first-line treatment. But even for methotrexate, evidence is limited to case reports and some case series. In a case series involving 7 patients for example, methotrexate significantly increased mean neutrophil count from 1.95 × 109/L to 3.92 × 109/L within 1 year. Neutrophil counts already started to rise after 4 weeks of treatment (68). Additionally, studies also reported a decline in infection rate (69) and beneficial effects on the healing of vasculitis-mediated ulcers (69). The efficacious methotrexate dose in most case reports and case series was 7.5 mg, which is very low when compared to usual doses used nowadays in rheumatology. Due to limited data, frequencies of treatment success and treatment failure are unknown. One case report actually reported a further decline in neutrophil count due to methotrexate (70), highlighting the importance of regular laboratory monitoring during therapy.
    If there is no adequate increase in neutrophil count under methotrexate therapy, the next step is to add rituximab (71–75). The pathogenetic considerations underpinning its choice implicate the presumed role of antibodies (e.g., ACPA, anti-neutrophil antibodies, anti-G-CSF antibodies) and B-cells in Felty’s syndrome. B-cells interact with T-cells via cytokines and antigen presentation and B-cell depletion using rituximab might thus have beneficial effects in Felty’s syndrome as well. A systematic review of case reports revealed normalization of neutrophil counts in 62.5% of cases after the first rituximab course. In most cases, this was paralleled by declining infection rates too (76). Usually, rituximab is administered as an intravenous infusion of 1,000 mg, repeated once 2 weeks later. But case reports also showed efficacy for the rituximab regime used in vasculitis treatment (i.e., weekly doses of 375 mg/m2 body surface area for 4 doses) (76). Depending on RA activity and neutrophil count, repeated rituximab courses may be needed. To date, long-term data concerning rituximab maintenance therapy in Felty’s syndrome is sparse, but Brockbank et al. reported sustained remission in one patient after 6 years of follow-up (74). However, some reports also pointed out rituximab-refractory cases, in which RA activity declined after 6 months of treatment, but without improving neutrophil count or infection rate (77). But it should be mentioned in this regard, that the patients involved in these case reports had been treated unsuccessfully with various DMARDs (disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs, i.e., methotrexate, sulfasalazine, hydroxychloroquine, leflunomide) as well as TNFα inhibitors before the start of rituximab. Therefore, the population studied was very difficult-to-treat, a fact that should be taken into consideration while interpreting study results.
    Another treatment option in the case of methotrexate/rituximab failure or adverse reactions is abatacept. There are two case reports demonstrating neutrophil normalization in rituximab-naive patients using intravenous or subcutaneous administration of abatacept (78, 79).
    In two case reports, cortisone-free neutrophil normalization over a period of 2 years was achieved using hydroxychloroquine (HCQ). Interestingly, neutrophil counts correlated with HCQ metabolites in whole blood and whole blood levels below target were associated with recurrent neutropenia. The target level was defined using the sum of two HCQ metabolites, i.e., desethylchloroquine (DCQ) and desethylhydroxychloroquine (DHCQ), measured in whole blood (target level: DCQ + DHCQ >1,000 ng/mL) (80). To achieve this target level, markedly higher HCQ doses are necessary (up to 1,200 mg HCQ per day), which are otherwise unusual in rheumatology because of concerns regarding ocular toxicity.
    There are a few case reports showing some efficacy of cyclosporin A as well. But in most cases, patients also received other immunosuppressants [e.g., hydroxychloroquine (81)] and methylprednisolone (4–6 mg per day) at the same time, making it hard to figure out efficacy of cyclosporin A alone. But at least, tapering of methylprednisolone was possible under cyclosporin A treatment (81–83).
    Data on leflunomide are conflicting. While Talip et al. reported an increase in neutrophil counts and the possibility of stopping glucocorticoid treatment under leflunomide (starting with leflunomide 100 mg per day for 3 days, followed by 20 mg per day), other case reports did not show any significant efficacy (72, 84).
    Until now, TNFα inhibitors like etanercept, infliximab and adalimumab have not shown any significant improvement in neutrophil counts in several case reports, although synovitis has been treated successfully with these agents (71, 72, 85, 86). In this context it should be noted, that immunosuppressants with proven efficacy in Felty’s syndrome should be used in any case, even if arthritis is well controlled by the current treatment. Thus, a change in treatment might be necessary (e.g., addition of methotrexate to an existing TNFα inhibitor therapy or switch to rituximab).
    Regarding tocilizumab, there is just one case report showing efficacy in Felty’s syndrome (87) and concerning JAK (Janus kinase) inhibitors, there is no data yet. But JAK inhibitors might turn out to be a promising treatment option, as tofacitinib and upadacitinib improved neutropenia in patients with rheumatoid arthritis accompanied by T-LGL leukemia (88, 89). The studied population was difficult-to-treat and various treatments (including methotrexate, rituximab, cyclosporin A, cyclophosphamide) had failed before starting a JAK inhibitor. Tofacitinib was linked to apoptosis of T-LGLs in vitro, especially in the context of existing STAT3 mutations (88). As mentioned above, T-LGLs as well as STAT3 mutations might play a role in at least some cases of Felty’s syndrome too. Considering these common pathomechanisms, JAK inhibitors might prove useful in the treatment of Felty’s syndrome in the future.
    Because it takes several weeks for immunosuppressants to have an effect, glucocorticoids and G-CSF are necessary as bridging. Glucocorticoids rapidly improve synovitis as well as neutropenia. Glucocorticoids increase neutrophils through acceleration of cell maturation, mobilization of neutrophils from the bone marrow and the marginal pool, reduction of leukocyte extravasation and by prolonging neutrophil survival via anti-apoptotic signaling (90).
    However, in Felty’s syndrome, prednisolone doses ≥30 mg per day are often necessary to improve neutropenia significantly (25, 37) and doses below 7.5 mg per day usually fail to sustain stable neutrophil counts in the absence of additional immunosuppressive treatment. This is further complicated by the fact that some patients with Felty’s syndrome do not respond to glucocorticoids adequately (25, 37) and that glucocorticoids should be avoided in the presence of active infection. To improve neutrophil counts rapidly in the presence of infection, administration of G-CSF is useful. In patients with Felty’s syndrome, filgrastim and lenograstim were used in particular. G-CSF dosage and frequency of administration depend on neutrophil count. An ANC target level of >1,500/μL can be recommended. In a case series of Stanworth et al., frequency of administration varied between once a day and two times a week. Standard doses as well as reduced doses of filgrastim (300 μg and 150 μg respectively) and lenograstim (263 μg and 105 μg respectively) were used over a period of up to 3.5 years. Adverse reactions (severe arthralgia, cutaneous vasculitis) only occurred at the beginning of the standard dose regime, thus starting with reduced doses and a gradual dose escalation can be recommended (91). Overall, G-CSF therapy seems to be safe over a considerable period of time.

    Conclusion
    Felty’s syndrome often comes along with diagnostic challenges, as there are many conditions/diseases with similar manifestations. To rule out malignancies, bone marrow examination is usually required. Although neutropenia with ensuing infections is still the main cause of death in Felty’s syndrome, other sequelae like non-cirrhotic portal hypertension leading to gastrointestinal bleeding as well as an increased lymphoma risk contribute to its morbidity and should be therefore kept in mind as well. Methotrexate and rituximab are still the mainstay of treatment and in most cases, Felty’s syndrome is sufficiently controlled by these drugs. But there are difficult-to-treat cases, necessitating new treatment options. Maybe JAK inhibitors might prove useful in the future, as there have been promising results in patients with T-LGL leukemia, which seems to share some pathomechanisms with Felty’s syndrome.

    termed ‘super rheumatoid’ disease.7 This concept was further proven with its very high
    association with HLA DR4 antigen.10,11 Interestingly, despite the severe joint
    destruction, synovitis is often mild or totally inactive.8,9 The most important
    consequence of neutropenia is the increased rate of infection6,13 – 15, leading to
    increased mortality.8,16 With respect to these features, proper diagnosis and treatment
    are very important because recurring bacterial infections can be effectively controlled.
    Interestingly, in 30% of FS patients there is evidence of large granular lymphocyte (LGL)
    expansion.17 Similar LGL expansion has been observed in a percentage of RA
    patients without FS.4,18 – 22 This association is also called pseudo-Felty syndrome.23
    These two entities have very similar immunogenetics, but this is not true for LGL
    expansion without arthritis.24
    In this chapter we describe and analyse the clinical and immunological features, the
    association with large granular lymphocyte syndrome, the immunogenetics and the
    course, outcome and treatment of this rare disease.
    CLINICAL FEATURES
    A family history of RA is more common in FS patients than in RA controls.8,25,26 The
    polyarthritis presenting in FS is of the rheumatoid type, and FS classically develops after a
    longer course of rheumatoid arthritis.3,6,9 Palindromic rheumatism as a prodrome of FS
    has been also described.27 Up to 1998, only three girls with juvenile rheumatoid arthritis
    (JRA) who developed FS had been reported.28 There are two more reports of
    patients with JRA persisting into adulthood who then developed FS.7,29 Interestingly,
    active synovitis is less commonly seen.6,8,9,16 Out of 100 FS patients seen at the
    Mayo Clinic during a 5-year period, 35 required no treatment because of lack of
    symptoms.30 In spite of the relatively mild or even absent synovitis, radiologically
    detectable joint destruction is severe.9,12 Campion et al followed up their FS patient for
    5.2 (0.3–14) years.8 During this period the percentage of patients with active synovitis
    had fallen from50–30%, while the radiology indexworsened considerably. This supports
    the hypothesis that the activity of synovitis and the severity of joint destructions are
    not associated tightly and may be governed by different factors.31 Coakley and colleagues
    hypothesized that interleukin-10—described as a factor that inhibits the synthesis of
    cytokines—may have a role in changing the disease phenotype from RA to FS, inhibiting
    joint inflammation. However, they could not find any association between FS and IL-10
    promoter polymorphism.32
    Studies of the synovium of FS patients are lacking; we were not able to find any
    histological studies in the literature. Even synovial fluid analysis was mentioned in only
    one of the studies.30 The authors analysed the synovial fluid of both knees of one
    patient. Cultures were negative; the cell counts were 17 000 and 8200, with 55 and 71%
    of polymorphonuclear cells, respectively. Cases of Felty’s syndrome with no joint
    syndrome were also described.33 – 35
    Neutropenia is certainly the most common and most important feature of FS.
    Leukopenia can be defined as a leukocyte count of less than 3 £ 109/l, with an absolute
    632 G. P. Balint and P. V. Balint
    neutrophil count of less than 2.0 £ 109/l8,36, but there was a higher incidence of bacterial
    infections with neutrophil counts ,0.5 £ 109/l.8 The degree of neutropenia varies with
    time, and even spontaneous remission may occur.16,37 Because a number of authors
    could not find any clinical differences between patients with the classical triad of Felty’s
    syndrome and patients with RA and otherwise unexplained neutropenia, even
    without splenomegaly, Bowman et al have suggested that this latter entity should also
    be called FS.21,38
    A subset of RA patients with neutropenia and LGL expansions—infrequently
    with splenomegaly—has also been described.4,19 Similar LGL expansion occurs in
    30–40% of FS patients4,22, although some authors could not find any LGL
    expansions in their FS patients.3,8 The relationship of FS and LGL expansion to RA
    is unclear. Some authors regard both entities as FS also on the basis of similar
    immunogenetics;17,21,22 others—regarding the somewhat different clinical features—
    think that these are distinct diseases.4,19 LGL expansion with RA is also called
    pseudo-FS.23 We discuss the similarities and differences between FS and pseudo-FS
    later in this chapter.
    There is an increased frequency of bacterial infections in FS.6,13,15,35 Neutropenia is
    believed to be the main cause of this increased rate of infection.8,14,36 The FS patients
    who develop recurrent infections have significantly lower neutrophil counts, usually
    ,0.5 £ 109/l.8,36 Other risk factors for infections include severe disability, a high level of
    immune complexes, hypocomplementaemia, neuropathy, skin ulcers, secondary
    Sjo¨gren syndromes and steroid treatment.2,8,15 Impaired phagocytosis and intracellular
    killing39, impaired chemotaxis40 and superoxide production41 may also contribute to the
    risk of recurrent infections. The prior ingestion of immunocomplexes may be
    responsible for impaired phagocytosis.23,39 Most often the infections affect the skin,
    mouth, and upper and lower respiratory tract.8 Increased mortality in FS is also probably
    due to an increased rate of infection. Out of the 32 FS patients of Campion et al, eight died
    during the mean 5.2 years (range 0.3–14.8) of follow-up, five of fulminant
    bronchopneumonia.8 Other authors found that the mortality of FS patients is not
    higher than that of RA patients3 or RA patients with severe extra-articular disease.42
    Neutrophil counts may return to normal without or after splenectomy, but even the
    patients with a normal neutrophil count may continue to have an increased rate of
    infection.6,8,29
    Anaemia is not different from the chronic anaemia of RA patients. Haemolytic
    anaemia may occur.6 Total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) is low6, and iron stores are
    absent.6,7 Thrombocytopenia is often present.6 – 8,13
    Splenomegaly is one of the features of the triad.1 The size of the spleen is variable;
    sometimes it is not large enough to be palpable.14 In obese patients—uncharacteristic
    in FS—ultrasound, radioisotope scanning and computed tomography (CT) can be used
    for detecting the spleen and assessing its size.4 It has been suggested that splenomegaly
    is not required for diagnosing FS; rheumatoid arthritis and unexplained neutropenia may
    fulfil the criteria for FS.21,38
    Splenic enlargement may occur even as a feature of RA without neutropenia, or as a
    coincidence of RA and liver cirrhosis, infectious diseases, septicaemia, amyloidosis,
    malignant haemoblastosis and other haematological diseases. Other articular diseases
    may be associated with enlarged spleen: Still’s disease, adult-onset Still’s disease,
    systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), articular brucellosis, chronic bacterial endocarditis
    etc. RA with splenomegaly but without unexplained neutropenia is not different from
    the usual RA.14 Mild hepatomegaly is common. Elevated levels of alkaline phosphatase
    and transaminase are described in 25% of the cases.8
    Felty’s syndrome 633
    An unusual type of liver involvement can be associated with FS.43 Histologically it is
    described as nodular regenerative hyperplasia, but its pathogenesis is not understood.44
    Thorne et al found histological abnormalities of the liver in 12 of 17 FS patients: five of
    regenerative hyperplasia, five of portal fibrosis. Four patients had portal hypertension,
    three with bleeding from oesophageal varices.44
    An increased risk of malignancies with a twofold increase of total cancer incidence is
    also a feature of FS.45 The increased risk of non-Hodgkin lymphoma is similar to the risk
    associated with Sjo¨gren syndrome (SS) and may be explained by the high association of FS
    with SS.45
    Other extra-articular features are also common. Pinals has summarized the extraarticular
    features, reviewing ten reports since 1962.5 The summarized percentages he
    calculated were the following: rheumatoid nodules 76%, weight loss 68%, secondary
    Sjo¨gren’s syndrome 56%, lymphadenopathy 34%, leg ulcers 25%, pleuritis 19%, skin
    pigmentation 17%, neuropathy 17%, episcleritis 8%. Campion et al, in their series of 32 FS
    patients, noticed that 91% of their patients had at least one extra-articular feature
    besides splenomegaly.8 They found hepatomegaly in 21%, pyrexia in 22%, vasculitis
    separate from leg ulcers in 28%, pulmonary fibrosis in 27%, and secondary Sjo¨ gren’s
    syndrome in 53%. Hyperpigmentation can be diffuse or localized onto the extensor
    surface of the lower leg, where stasis and extravasation of red blood cells may be due to
    small vessel disease. Neuropathy and steroid-induced skin fragility are often associated
    with leg ulcers.23
    IMMUNOLOGICAL FEATURES
    Rheumatoid factor positivity can be detected in 95–100% of the patients, generally in
    high titres.6–9 ANA positivity is also common, occurring in 47–100% of the patients.2,8
    Not only IgM RF, but also IgG RF and IgA RF were found.6,46 Antihistone antibodies are
    present in 68–83%.47 In contrast to antihistone antibodies present in SLE, their
    reactivity was lost when histones were denatured in detergent.8 Antibodies
    against dsDNA rarely occur8, but antibodies against ssDNA can frequently be
    detected.14 No anti-SM and, interestingly, no anti-Ro, anti-LA or anti-U-RNP
    antibodies can be found despite the high incidence of associated Sjo¨ gren’s syndrome.8
    Immunoglobulin levels are higher than in the other RA patients9 and complement levels
    are lower.7 Levels of circulating immune complex (CIC) are usually high.48 – 50
    The causes of neutropenia were reviewed by Rosenstein and Kramer.23
    Theoretically, neutropenia may result from (1) impaired granulocytopoiesis,
    (2) peripheral destruction and increased sequestration, (3) excessive margination
    of neutrophils, or (4) varying combinations of these three possibilities.
    Bone marrow examination most commonly shows normal or increased myeloid
    cellularity with maturation arrest.8,51,52 Reduced granulocyte mass and mitotic pools
    were also reported.53 Impaired granulopoiesis may result from:
    1. cell-mediated suppression of myeloid precursors; peripheral and bone marrow
    T cells54—especially cortisol-sensitive T cells52, but also monocytes and other
    mononuclear leukocytes—may inhibit granulopoiesis in the marrow54,55
    2. decreased colony-stimulating activity was found in the serum and urine51 and
    in peripheral blood leukocytes2
    3. humoral suppression of normal granulopoiesis by non-immunglobulin humoral
    factors was also reported56
    634 G. P. Balint and P. V. Balint
    Peripheral destruction and increased sequestration of neutrophils also occurs.
    Serum antibodies against mature neutrophils were also found57, but it was shown that
    the Fab fragments of these antibodies are incapable of binding to neutrophils.50,58
    Granulocyte-bound IgGs 49,55,59,60 and also increased IgG neutrophil-binding activity49,60
    were shown in FS patients, with a strong correlation with each other.55 However, neither
    the amount of granulocyte-bound IgGs nor the IgG neutrophil-binding capacity
    correlated with neutrophil counts.55 Not only IgG but IgA and IgM can be also bound,
    but F(ab)2 fragments of IgG fromFS sera did not bind to granulocytes.50 The PMN-binding
    capacity is due to binding of immunocomplexes containing these immunoglobulins and
    not to the presence of autoantibodies directed to antigens or the neutrophil surface.
    PMN-bound IgG may cause neutropenia either by more rapid removal of neutrophils
    from circulation or by increased margination.49 PMN-bound IgG may lead to cell
    dysfunction without necessarily inducing neutropenia.49 Shortened survival61 and
    excessive margination of neutrophils were also reported.62 Marked elevation of serum
    neutrophil-binding IgGmay predict the response of splenectomy, and postoperative fall is
    associated with response.60 Sulphasalazine treatment also normalized granulocytebound
    IgG.63 Coremans et al found antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCAs) in
    77% of their 30 FS patients.64 The fluorescence pattern of ANCA was non-specific.
    Increased reactivity against lactoferrin (LF) was detected in 50% of their FS patients,
    compared to 4% in rheumatoid controls. In the authors’ opinion ANCAs—particularly
    those directed to LF—may contribute to neutropenia in FS: ANCAs may activate
    neutrophils, causing increased adherence to endothelial cells65, or may cross-react with
    epitopes of cytoplasmic antigens expressed on both the neutrophil and endothelial
    surfaces, leading to increased margination of neutrophils.64 Both humoral and cellular
    immune mechanisms are involved in the neutropenia of FS patients. Some of
    Starkebaum’s patients had increased IgG neutrophil-binding activity while in the other
    patients peripheral blood mononuclear cells inhibited the normal marrow granulocyte
    colony growth. There was no overlap between these two mechanisms in their patients.55
    LARGE GRANULAR LYMPHOCYTE EXPANSION, FELTY’S SYNDROME,
    RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS
    Large granular lymphocytes (LGLs) are a distinct subset of peripheral blood
    mononuclear cells representing 5–10% of the circulating population. These are cells
    12–20 mm in diameter which have pale blue cytoplasm, eccentric nuclei and prominent
    azurophilic granules. On histochemical staining they are positive for acid phosphatase
    and negative formyeloperoxidase, suggesting a lymphoid origin.66 LGLs are either CD32
    natural killer cells or CD3þ activated cytotoxic T cells.67,68 Homogeneous expansion of
    LGLs is a low-grade malignant disease which is also called chronic
    T g-lymphoproliferative disease or chronic T-cell lymphocytosis.69 It is associated with
    neutropenia, anaemia and sometimes thrombocytopenia. Splenomegaly is common;
    lymphocytosis of the peripheral blood and bone marrow is a characteristic finding.18,70
    Granular lymphocyte proliferation can be defined as blood lymphocytosis with a
    lymphocyte count .4.5 £ 109/l or a marrow lymphocyte count of more than 23.8% of
    nucleated cells.4
    CD3þ LGLs (T-LGLs) and CD32 LGLs (NK-LGLs) can be distinguished.68 The clonal
    nature of NK-LGLs is controversial.71 The more common T-LGLs can easily be
    detected by showing clonal rearrangement of TCR.20 Non-clonal LGL expansion is
    usually of chronic reactive origin. T-LGL expansion can be caused and sustained by
    Felty’s syndrome 635
    chronic viral infections such as CMV72, HTLV-1 or HIV infections73,74 and may also
    follow splenectomy or bone marrow transplantation.75
    The main symptoms of T-LGL lymphocytosis are constitutional symptoms such as
    fatigue, weight loss, fever and night sweats. The rate of infection of 15% is lower than
    would be expected from the neutropenia.20 Splenomegaly, neuropathy (especially
    mononeuritis multiplex), lymphadenopathy, polyendocrinopathy and oral ulcers may
    also appear.20
    The lymphocyte phenotypic abnormalities were similar to those observed in
    T g-lymphoproliferative diseases: inverted CD4þ:CD8þ cell ratio, increase of cells
    co-expressing CD8 and CD57 antigens and activated T (CD3þ/DRþ) cells.76 The most
    common phenotype of these cells is CD2þ, CD3þ, CD42, CD8þ, CD16þ and
    CD57þ.17,19,20 Interestingly, in eight patients of Gonzales-Chambers with proven clonal
    expansion of LGLs, none had blood or marrow lymphocytosis characteristic of LGL
    syndrome. The authors suppose that these RA patients with neutropenia have a
    subclinical disease resembling T g-lymphoproliferative diseases.76
    A surprisingly high percentage (20–30%) of LGL syndrome is associated with
    rheumatoid arthritis.19 In contrast, only 0.6% of RA patients have been reported to have
    T g-lymphoproliferative diseases.4 Saway et al found that 18 out of their 1053 RApatients
    had otherwise unexplained neutropenia. Twelve patients had FS, and six had LGL
    syndromes with RA; this represents 20% of the LGL syndrome patients documented in
    their institution. No patient with FS and RA had granular lymphocyte proliferation
    without neutropenia.
    Bowman et al studied LGL syndrome in patients with FS. In their 47 patients with FS,
    19% had clear evidence for LGL expansions, while 42% had variable evidence. Of these
    T-cell expansions 76% were clonal, demonstrated by Southern blotting and analysis with
    TCR b-chain content region probes. No evidence for shared Vb usage among
    expressants was seen, suggesting the absence of a role for superantigens.17
    LGL syndrome with arthritis shows identical class II association with FS; HLA DRB1*
    0401 was found in 78% of FS patients (odds ratio 27.9) and 83% of LGL patients with
    rheumatoid arthritis (odds ratio 35.4) compared to11% of controls. There were
    subtle immunogenetic differences between the two syndromes in the class I region. The
    class I association can be explained by linkage disequilibrium between DRB1*0401 and
    A*2, B* 44, Cw*0501.24 There was no association between HLA class I or class II
    histocompatibility antigen and LGL without arthritis.21 Immunogenetics studies show
    that LGL with rheumatoid arthritis and FS—regardless of whether or not it is
    associated with LGL expansion—constitutes a subset of rheumatoid arthritis having a
    stronger genetic basis than is found in other cases of RA.17,21,22,24 Clinically, however,
    there are differences between FS and LGL with RA.4,19,20,23 The clinical and laboratory
    differences are summarized in Table 1.
    Regarding the similarities and differences between the two entities, Rosenstein and
    Kramer suggested that the syndrome of LGL and RA should be called pseudo-FS.
    There are no studies, however, analysing whether there are any differences in
    symptomatology between FS with or without LGL syndrome. The clonal LGL expansion
    in FS and other cases in RA may raise speculations about the unknown pathogenesis of
    RA. Simultaneous occurrence of both LGL proliferation and RA in some patients may
    suggest common aetiologies.23 The suspicion that the monoclonal lymphocyte
    proliferation may be responsible for development of the inflammatory arthritis was
    suggested by the similarities to the MRL murine line, which develops a rheumatoid-like
    arthritis in the presence of the 1pr (lymphoproliferation) gene.77 This mouse model of
    autoimmune disease is also characterized by massive T-cell proliferation and abnormal

    COURSE AND OUTCOME
    Most authors are of the opinion that Felty’s syndrome has a poorer prognosis than
    uncomplicated RA. This is reflected by increased mortality caused by recurrent
    infection, with a 5-year mortality rate of up to 36%.8,15,16 Thomas et al, in their 20-year
    follow-up national study of the cause-specific mortality in RA, could find only 76 FS
    patients out of their more than 33 000 RA patients; this number was too small and the
    confidence intervals for a standardized mortality rate were too wide to draw any
    conclusions about mortality of FS.84
    In the only community-based study of extra-articular manifestations of RA, virtually
    all the excess mortality occurred in patients with severe extra-articular
    disease, including FS.42 Sibley et al, in their case-control study, could not find any
    difference in mortality, not even in mortality due to infections between FS and RA
    patients.3 Cardiovascular disease was the most common cause of death in both groups.
    Spontaneous remissions do occur in FS also in non-splenectomized patients.16,37
    In 50% of the patients of Thorne et al without splenectomy there was a similar increase
    in white blood cell counts at the end of follow-up.16 Sibley et al think that the prognosis
    of FS is not as poor as had been reported earlier.3
    TREATMENT
    Splenectomy
    Rashba et al summarized the retrospective results of six studies from the literature,
    including 118 patients who had undergone splenectomy. Only patients with the classical
    triad and with marrow biopsy showing hypercellular marrow with maturation arrest
    were included. Patients with marrow biopsies with T g-lymphocytosis were excluded
    as this represents a disorder distinct from Felty’s syndrome. The haematological
    response was defined as a permanent rise of absolute neutrophil count by 1000 per ml.
    The haematological response rate was 80% regardless of whether the indication was
    recurrent infections or only neutropenia.
    Patients who do not respond haematologically have a higher rate of non-fatal
    infections, but a significant minority (46%) do not experience any further infections. Of
    the patients who had infections prior to surgery, 55% did not experience further
    infection after splenectomy. The incidence of fatal infections was 12% regardless of
    whether a haematological response occurred or not. The authors assessed splenectomy
    as an effective method for the treatment of neutropenia and recurrent infections.85 It was
    found that an increase in granulocyte-bound IgG and serum granulocyte-binding
    638 G. P. Balint and P. V. Balint
    IgG might predict a response to splenectomy, and the increased level fell after
    operations.59,60 Low levels, however, do not exclude a good response.
    After splenectomy, three of the six non-responder patients of Logue exhibited
    antibodies that caused in vitro lysis of granulocytes by lymphocytes (antibody-dependent
    lymphocyte-mediated granulocyte toxicity). These antibodies were not present in
    any patients pre-operatively.59 The suspicion was raised that unsuccessfully splenectomized
    patients reported earlier might have LGL syndrome with RA13,18 because in this
    syndrome splenectomy has an adverse effect. Splenic embolization may be an alternative
    to splenectomy.86 There are no data about perioperative mortality and the effectiveness
    of vaccination in preventing infections.85
    Colony-stimulating factors
    The rationale for this treatment was the observation of Gupta et al, who noticed that
    the serum of FS patients had reduced colony-stimulating activity.51 In 1995 Moore et al
    reviewed 16 patients treated with growth factors—five with G-CSF and 11 with
    GM-CSF, including their own case.87 They noticed a sustained granulopoietic response
    in some cases.
    Stanworth et al treated eight FS patients with neutropenia ,1 £ 109/l with G-CSF.
    All patients had severe recurrent infections or required joint replacement surgery.
    The authors used two different G-CSF preparations: filgrastim (300 mg/ml) and
    lenograstim (263 or 105 mg/ml). In two patients the G-CSF treatment has been started
    at full vial dosage daily or on alternate days. The treatment had to be stopped because
    of generalized malaise and joint pain in one case and severe vasculitic rash in the others.
    In three of the remaining six patients the treatment could be restarted at a reduced
    dose and with a change in the proprietary formulation. G-CFS raised the neutrophil
    count to 1 2 4.5 £ 109/l, the rate of severe infections, and surgery could have been
    performed without infective complications. The long-term treatment did not
    exacerbate arthritis. Significant side-effects occurred in five patients, including nausea,
    malaise, generalized pains and vasculitic rash. One of three patients whose FS was
    associated with large granular T-cell lymphocytosis showed a reduction in this subset of
    lymphocytes during G-CSF treatment.88
    Disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs)
    Wassenberg et al reported the biggest series (seven patients) of FS patients treated with
    methotrexate (MTX), analysing the results retrospectively. They also reviewed the
    previous reports. All of their patients had splenomegaly and erosive rheumatoid arthritis.lo
    All but one was RF-positive. All the patients had granulocytopenia during the course of
    their disease, although at the time of the study three patients had .2.0 £ 109/l
    granulocytes. The observation period was 1 year. The mean dose of MTX was
    12.8 mg/week. The granulocytopenia significantly improved from a mean of 1952–3922.
    The number of swollen joints decreased significantly from 19 to 7.4, while the
    erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) decreased from 40.9 to 26.6 mm/hour. The daily
    dose of prednisone could be reduced from 1.75 to 0.96 after 1 year of treatment. The
    authors reviewed ten earlier reports, including 14 patients altogether. Twoof them could
    not be assessed; in one case the patient was treated for only 1 month, in the other case no
    details were available. The usual dose ofMTX was 7.5 mg perweek.89 Puechal et al found
    a dramatic increase of neutrophils within 4 days after intravenous infusion of MTX.90
    Gerster treated his patient for 12 years with MTX. He reported sustained efficacy of
    Felty’s syndrome 639
    clinical signs, although there was a decrease in granulocyte count after 4 years. The
    granulocytopenia normalized; in all the other cases the ESR and the swollen joint counts
    also improved, and in all cases they were measured.91
    Dillon et al treated the largest series (20 patients) with parenteral gold. All patients
    suffered from infections or other complications (i.e. infections, vasculitis,
    lymphadenopathy, weight loss etc) of FS. Some 60% of their patients had a complete
    response, 20% had partial response and 20% were unresponsive by entry criteria.
    No serious complications were encountered. Dillon et al also reviewed the reports
    about FS patients receiving gold treatment. Out of the 34 patients they could collect,
    88% had partial or complete response, three failed to respond, and one died of nondrug-
    related complications.30
    Of the other DMARDS, cyclophosphamide92,93, cyclosporine94,95, d-penicillamine96,
    leflunomide97, and sulphasalazine63 were also reported to have favourable results.
    Sulphasalazine caused significant reduction in neutrophil-bound IgG.63
    Of the other treatment modalities reported in the literature, in the review of Rashba
    et al corticosteroids, intravenous immunoglobulin, plasmapheresis and prophylactic
    antibiotics were regarded as of unproven benefit, and therapies with lithium and
    testosterone as having potential efficacy.85 These last two drugs have not been used in the
    last 15 years.
    No other biological therapies, other than colony-stimulating factors, have been used.
    Only Talip et al mentioned that their patient, favourably treated by leflunomide,
    had earlier received etanercept without success.97
    Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)
    Surprisingly, in the newer literature there is no mention of whether NSAIDs can be or
    should be used for controlling arthritis in FS. In our opinion NSAIDs are better avoided
    because of their potency for worsening neutropenia.
    Algorithm of treatment
    Studying the literature, we share the opinion of Rashba et al85 that the first choice for
    treating both neutropenia and arthritis is MTX or perhaps other DMARDs. If this kind of
    treatment is unsuccessful, and there is an increased rate of infection or planned joint
    surgery, then colony-stimulating factor treatment with a ‘go low, go slow’strategy should
    be tried, with splenectomy at the end of the treatment algorithm. Quick improvement
    in neutropenia has been reported not only after splenectomy but also with
    intravenous MTX90 and colony stimulating factor treatment.87
    SUMMARY
    FS syndrome is still an intriguing subset of RA. The high percentage of RF, ANA and
    DRB4*0401 positivity, severe joint destruction and extra-articular disease are the
    essential features of this ‘super-rheumatoid’ disease. Splenomegaly is no longer regarded
    as an essential part of the syndrome; unexplained neutropenia with RA satisfies the
    criteria for FS. Interestingly, another syndrome—LGL expansion and rheumatoid
    arthritis with similar neutropenia (but not leucopenia) and splenomegaly—has also been
    described and called pseudo-Felty syndrome. This syndrome is very similar
    640 G. P. Balint and P. V. Balint
    immunogenetically, but is clinically different. LGL expansion in 30% of FS patients can also
    be found, but the significance of this finding is unknown.
    The clinical significance of FS lies in the fact that the often inactive joint disease
    diverts the clinician’s attention from the severe extra-articular disease and neutropenia
    causing recurrent, sometimes fatal infections.
    Treatment of neutropenia and the recurrent infections of FS includes DMARDs,
    especially MTX, and treatment with granulocyte colony stimulating factor. The effect of
    biological treatments is not known. Splenectomy improves neutropenia and, to a lesser
    degree, the incidence of recurrent infections. LGL syndrome with RA may respond to
    low-dose MTX treatment and prednisone. Splenectomy has no effect, and may even
    cause deterioration.
    Practice points
    † rheumatoid patients with unexplained, continuous neutropenia should be
    suspected of having Felty’s syndrome, even in the absence of splenomegaly
    † patients with rheumatoid arthritis and splenomegaly without previous or
    present unexplained neutropenia should not be regarded as having Felty’s
    syndrome, even if no other cause of splenomegaly can be found
    † even when the arthritis of an FS patient is inactive, patients with unexplained
    neutropenia should be kept under close supervision
    † patients with unexplained, continuous neutropenia without arthritis but with a
    high level of rheumatoid factor should be suspected of developing Felty’s
    syndrome
    † although 30–40% of FS patients can have LGL expansion, the LGL expansion
    with RA or pseudo-FS may be a different syndrome
    † although there is also neutropenia and infrequent splenomegaly in LGL
    syndrome with RA, this condition is characterized by normal or elevated
    leukocyte count lymphocytosis and a lesser rate of recurrent infections
    † FS patients with an increased rate of infection or waiting for joint surgery
    certainly require suitable treatment even when their arthritis is inactive
    † the first choice of treatment of Felty’s syndrome with granulocytopenia and
    increased rate of infection should be low-dose MTX or other DMARDs
    † if DMARD treatment is unsuccessful, G-CSF is recommended on a ‘go low, go
    slow’ basis
    † for treatment-resistant cases splenectomy is indicated, if splenomegaly can be
    detected
    † splenectomy is contraindicated in LGL expansion with RA
    † there is no experience with biological treatment of FS other than G-CSF
    † NSAIDs are better avoided in controlling arthritis because of their potential to
    worsen neutropenia
    
    ## ***Evaluación de actividad de enfermedad:***
    
    *Existen varios score multivariables (TJC, SJC, evaluación global del paciente y del médico, evaluación del dolor, PCR/VHS), los exámenes de imágenes o paneles de citoquinas para el control de la enfermedad están en revisión.*
    
    - *DAS 28 (art dolorosas/inflam, PCR o VHS, eval global del pcte): remisión (<2.6), actv baja (> 2.6<3.2), moderada actv (>3.2<5.1), alta actv (>5.1)*
    - *SDAI (art inflam/dolorosas, PCR, eval global del pcte y del médico) con remisión <3.3*
    - *CDAI (art inflam/dolorosas, eval global del pcte y del médico)*
    - *Grado de incapacidad mediado por Stanford HAQ (encuesta sobre vestirse/asearse, levantarse, comer, caminar/pasear, Higiene personal, alcanzar objetos elevados, tomar objetos, otros).*
    - *Grado de erosión por score de Larsen y score de Sharp*
    
    *El conteo articular se basa en 28 en la AR debido a que el conteo de 44, 66 o 68 articulaciones cuenta articulaciones que no son comprometidas habitualmente por la AR (pudiendo dar falsos positivos). Esta postura no le quita importancia en la evaluación de otras articulaciones, las que se deben tener en consideración en el seguimiento. Las “tender” o dolorosas se relacionan con los cambios estructurales que puede tener esa articulación, las “swolen” o hinchadas se correlaciones con progreso del daño (reflejado mejor el proceso patológico de la AR) y relacionándose más con los reactantes de fase aguda.*
    
    *El VAS (100-mm) evalúa el dolor en la última semana, en donde la ACR recomienda la aplicación de la escala horizontal de 0-10 cms.*
    
    *La evaluación global de la enfermedad por el paciente (PGA) o del médico (EGA) se hace similar al VAS de 0-10 cms*
    
    *La monitorización inicial debe hacerse cada 3 meses para el ajuste de la terapia, la que debe ser modificada una vez logrado el objetivo (baja actividad o remisión).*
    
    *La monitorización de la actividad de la enfermedad se debe hacer en paralelo a la evaluación complementaria de la progresión de la enfermedad, daño articular, invalidez, manifestaciones extra-articulares y eventos adversos de la medicación.*
    
    ***Evaluación inicial previo a la terapia:***
    
    *Antes de iniciar la terapia son múltiples las variables que se debe evaluar. El nivel de actividad de la enfermedad, presencia de comorbilidades, estadio de la terapia (inicio vs resistencia), compromiso extra-articular, preferencias del paciente infecciones latentes y antecedentes de infecciones por año, vacunas, neoplasia, exámenes generales, descartar causas que pueden generar intolerancia gástrica porque se asocia a abandono de la terapia, cigarro, OH, conductas de riesgo o situaciones de riesgo infeccioso a futuro.*
    
    *Determinar los factores de mal pronóstico, siendo el compromiso extrarticular el mayor predictor de mortalidad en pacientes con AR (siendo 5 veces mayor) .*
    
    *Los factores de mal pronóstico se evalúan en base al daño articular, invalidez y mortalidad.*
    
    *Sumar al debut un DAS28 alto, erosiones en las imágenes (los pacientes que tienen erosiones lo más probable que haga más erosiones), cigarro, recuento articular a los factores de mal pronóstico.*

## Tratamiento

ObjetivoS: disminuir dolor, controlar inflamación articular, mejorar movilidad articular, evitar progresión radiológica, mejorar calidad de vida, minimizar los efectos adversos, prevenir y controlar comorbilidades y reducir la mortalidad.

**Terapia enfocada en los objetivos** (T2T, treat to target): se busca **alcanzar la remisión**, o , de no ser posible, una baja actividad de la enfermedad en el menor tiempo posible. Ajustando la terapia hasta llegar al objetivo.  

Se sugiere iniciar un fármaco, vigilando los efectos adversos cada ***4 semanas*** hasta objetivos. Si a los **3 meses no se logrado reducir actividad inicial de la enfermedad, se considera cambiar el fármaco. Si a los 6 meses no se ha logrado objetivo, modificar terapia.**

Remisión (DAS <2.6) o baja actividad de la enfermedad (<3.2).

- No fumar

Las intervenciones educacionales reportan beneficio en el autoaprendizaje y en las terapias cognitivo-conductuales en relación al control del dolor en relación a intervención psíquicos y ambientales en el dolor.*

- ***Nutrición:***
    
    *AR activa se asocia a anorexia y mala alimentación.* 
    
- ***Comorbilidades:***
    
    *Manejo DLP, HTA y sedentarismo.*

### **Tratamiento farmacológico**

- **AINES, sintomático**
- **Corticoides:** limitar su uso a un máximo de 6 meses hasta que el DMARDs logre su mejor acción. Se recomienda una dosis baja de uso ( PDN 7.5 mg/día, idealmente bajo 5 mg al día).
    
    Independiente de la dosis, se asocia a ***infecciones, osteoporosis y diabetes.***
    
- **DMARDs:**
    - **Metotrexato:** Fármaco de elección. Efectos en 6 a 8 semanas. ***Baja actividad como monoterapia 30%, algunos dicen que hasta 60% a los 6 meses***, donde se observa su máxima eficacia.
        
        Contraindicado en embarazo, OH, VHB/VHC, cirrosis. Relativa VFG<30, infección activa no en Síndrome de Felty
        
        Atención!: Baja actividad de las vacunas.
        
    - **Leflunomida:** Eficacia no inferior al MTX como monoterapia en control de síntomas, terapia alternativa al MTX. También puede ser utilizada en conjunto con iTNF-a o con MTX (aunque no existen estudios que avalen esta combinación). Comienzo de acción entre 6-8 semanas. **No usar con rifampicina**
    - **Sulfasalazina:** No es tan efectivo en la prevención del daño radiológico como monoterapia, pero con mejor resultado en terapia asociada. **Se puede utilizar en embarazo o lactancia**
    - **Hidroxicloroquina:** Se restringe a pacientes con poca actividad clínica y sin factores de mal pronóstico de enfermedad ya que su ***efecto modificador de enfermedad es débil.*** Su inicio de acción es lento, con su mejor efecto a los 2-4 meses.
    - **Ciclosporina:** Efecto de acción máximo entre 2-3 meses. La dosis utilizada es de 2.5 mg/Kg/día con incrementos de 0.5 mg/Kg/día cada 2 semanas hasta llegar a un máximo de 5 mg/Kg/día. Tiene efectos adversos como nefrotoxicidad, HTA, hipertricosis, hipertrofia gingival, parestesias, temblor. Evidencia?
    - **Azatioprina:** Se utiliza a dosis de 2-2.5 mg/Kg/día (25-50 mg/día en la primera semana para luego aumentar de 0.5 mg/kg cada 4-6 semanas. Evidencia?

    ***En AR el uso del primer bDMARDs logra una baja actividad de enfermedad en un 50% de los casos***

            - *ASPIRE: 1004 pctes, respuesta ACR 20, 50 70 a 54 semanas, Mejor respuesta IFX/MTX mas que en MTX solo en lo clínico, radiológico y funcional*
        - **Etanercept:** 50 mg/semanal, en combinación con MTX.
            - ETN vs placebo en ACR 20, 50, 70 a 3-6 meses → mejor respuesta en todos los campos evaluados.
        - **Adalimumab:** 40 mg cada 15 días.
            - *ARMADA: doble ciego, 24 semanas, 271 pctes aleatorizados a ADA (20mg, 40 mg, 80 mg) mas MTX o placebo mas MTX presentado mejor respuesta en todos los campos.*
            - *PREMIER: 799 pctes, AR reciente, activa y agresiva sin tratamiento previo con MTX en donde se comparó MTX/placebo, ADA 40 mg, ADA 40 mg + MTX. Se evidenció mejor respuesta en grupo ADA + MTX (remisión a dos años de un 49% por DAS28 < 2.6).*

***AR PRECOZ:***

- *TICORA (2004): Se comparó tratamiento intensivo vs terapia estándar para AR precoz en donde se busco una buena respuesta (DAS <2.4 o caída del basal >1.6) o remisión (DAS <1.6) a 18 meses de seguimiento. Se randomizaron 111 pcte (55 y 55) en donde el grupo control se hicieron controles mensuales con terapia escalonada (SSZ; SSZ/MTX/HCQ; Incremento de dosis; PDN 7.5 mg; cambio en triple terapia). Se encontro que la terapia intensiva mejora la actividad de la enfermedad, progresión de la enfermedad, funcionalidad, calidad de vida y progresión radiológica sin aumentar los costos totales.*
- *COBRA: MTX+SSZ+PDN 60 mg (Tap 7.5 mg en 6 semanas) VS SSF monoterapia. Presentó mejor resultado articular y funcionalidad a 5-10 años.*
- *COBRA Light: Lo mismo pero con PDN 30 mg, no encontrado diferencia con los resultados de estudio COBRA*
- *BEST: Estrategias de manejo de AR precoz: A) Monoterapia secuencial, B) terapia combinada progresiva, C) terapia combinada COBRA clásica, D) Terapia combinada IFX + MTX. Se encontro que estrategias C y D no mostraron diferencia entre sí (pero ambas son mejores que las estartegias A y B).*
- *t-REACH: Compara eficacia de (MTX+SSZ+HCQ+PDN 15 mg con Tap a 2.5 mg en 10 sem) VS (Dosis única de MTP 120 mg o Traimcinoilona 80 mg); la ídea es comprar indicación de corticoides en AR precoz. Al año sin difefencias en DAS, HAQ, RADAI. MTX + PDN en 3er brazo de estudio mostró resultados similares.*
- *CARE-RA: 90 pacientes de bajo riesgo (erosiones, FR/CCP, DAS PCR) aleatorizados a MTX monoterapia o con asociación de PDN pauta COBRA Light. No se encontraron diferencias significativas pero con tendencia a mejor DAS, HAQ en grupo con corticoides.*
- *CARDERA: 467 pacientes con AR precoz. MTX monoterapia, MTX + Csa, MTX + PDN 60, MTX + Csa + PDN 60. El objetivo del grupo con PDN es bajarlo a 7.5 mg en 6 semanas. Se encontraron mejores resultados en grupo MTX + PDN en DAS 28, Rx, HAQ y remisión por DAS 28 en seguimiento a 2 años*

***AR con fallo a DMARDs:***

- *TEAR: 755 pctes a triple terapia (MTX+HCQ+SSZ) VS (MTX + ETA) no mostró diferencia en DAS 28*
- *SWEFOT: 433 pcte a triple terapia VS MTX + IFX en donde se encotraron mejor respuesra a 12 meses en grupop MTX/IFX pero sin diferencias estadísticas*
- *IMPROVED: 610 pctes a triple terapia VS MTX+ADA a 8 meses sin diferencias significativas en DAS 28 pero en grupo MTX + ADA presentaron mas remisión por DAS 28 a los 2 años.*
- *En AR reciente, la triple terapia VS MTX+IFX son equivalentes*
- *AR reciente con fallo a MTX, la combinación MTX + IFX es mejor en Rx*
- *En AR establecida con respuesta insuficiente a MTX, la combinación MTX + IFX es mejor que la triple terapia*
- *Consideraciones: Parece que el beneficio de la triple terapia es mas lento que los biológicos, lo que puede llegar a ser relavante en AR con mal pronóstico (iniciar luego IFX + MTX por ejemplo). En pacientes con biológicos y contraindicación de MTX, se puede utilziar LFN como alternativa. Dosis de MTX > 10 mg/semanal se relacionba con niveles plasmáticos y AutoAc contra IFX y dosis de MTX entra 10-20 mg/semanal tienen la misma efectividad.*

- *RTX es igual que los anti-TNF (ADA,ETA) en ACR 20/50/70 y DAS 28.*

*La base del manejo es la**Terapia farmacológica**, en donde los estudios pivotales destaca:*

- ***TICORA** (2004): Manejo intensivo de la AR precoz con objetivo de control de la enfermedad. Es un estudio ciego simple randomizado y controlado de 111 pacientes donde se tomó como objetivo la caída en la actividad de la enfermedad y porcentaje de respuesta a tratamiento (DAS <2.4 o >1.2 de caída del basal). Se randomizo a un grupo de manejom intensivo (SSZ 40 mg/kg/día---SSZ + MTX (7.5 mg/sem) + HCQ (< 6.5 mg/kg/día) donde se le agregó PDN 7.5 mg/día con cambio a Csa (2-5 mg/kg/día) o LEF según escala de manejo con evaluación mensual VS grupo de manejo estándar para la fecha. Se evidenció que los paciente con manejo intensivo mejoraron en la actividad de la enfermedad, funcionalidad, calidad de vida sin mayores efectos advervos y sin mayores costos asociados.*

*La estrategia de menejo, gracias a los halalzgos en el estudio TICORA, es T2T (objetivo de remisión o de baja actividad)*

- ***Dosis de MTX** se aconseja de forma escalonada: ajustar 0.3 mg/Kg/sem cada 4-6 semanas. Estudio ESPOIR (2017) en una cohorte de AR precoz (estrategias de uso de MTX e impacto) llevándo a dosis óptima (10 mg/sem primeros 3 meses para luego llevarlo a > 20 mg/sem o 0.3 mg/kg/sem en el 6to mes) se evaluaron 314 pacientes en donde esta estrategia de uso fue mas eficaz en lograr respuesta a 1 y 2 años.*
- *Esquemas de bDMARDs + MTX VS MTX + GC en pacientes naive a MTX no ha mostrado superioridad (no se ha realizado estudio en donde se inicia con tsDMARDs en pacientes Naive).*
- *El uso de GC de forma abreviada al inicio de la enfermedad, como puente para lograr bajar la carga inflamatoria mientras ejerce su mejor acción el csDMARDs, con la idea de lograr la suspención de GC en un plazo de 6 meses a 1 año. En caso de no lograr el tapering de los GC se debe considerar fracaso de la terapia*
    - **NORD-Star Trial** (2019): tratamiento óptimo en AR precoz con MTX + GC VS bDMARDs (TCZ, CTZ, ABA) como randomizado multicéntrico con objetico de DAS < 2.8 a la semana 24. No se mostraron diferencias en los grupos
    - **COBRA** classic (1997): Randomizado step-down de PDN (60 mg son tapering precoz)+ MTX + SSZ VS SSZ monoterapia en AR precoz. Se siguieron a 155 pctes con AR precoz con objetivo de suspender PDN y MTX a ka semana 28-40 según el tratante. A la semana 28, la terapia combinada mostró mejores resultados, la diferencia se declina y se pierde luego de que la PDN fue suspendida (pero sin cambios cuando el MTX se suspende). En seguimientos posteriores del COBRA el beneficio del grupo con PDN en dosis altas se mantuvo en el tiempo (seguimiento a 1 y 2 años).
        - **COBRA slim** (PDN 30 mg) y **COBRA Avant Garde** (LEF) mostraron que dosis de corticoides mas bajas (30 mg) con tapering precoz fue igualmente efectivo que el protocolo COBRA classic.
    - **CARE Trail** (2015): combinación de COBRA Classic, COBRA Slim o COBRA Avant Garde en AR precoz (< 4 meses) en 16 semanas en pacientes de alto riesgo teniendo como objetivo LDA a la semana 8 y remisión a la semana 16 medido por DAS28. COBRA Slim mostró igual efectividad y con menor utilización de corticoides
    - **ESPOIR Cohorts** (2015): Cohorte francesa que ha hecho seguimientos varios en AR. Se demostró que el tiempo de los síntomas e inicio de tratamiento se asocia a malos resultados, de aquí se desprende el concepto de ventana de oportunidad. Esta no e suna relación lineal, pero se sugiere un periodo de tiempo en donde la respuesta sería mejor. El periodo óptimo seía dentro de las primeras 15 semanas de inicio de los síntomas (en caso de los sero-positivos, dentro de las primeras 11 semanas de inicio de los síntomas).
    - **BEST** (2005): evaluación de los resultados clínico y radiológicos en AR precoz para resultados a largo plazo. Es un estudio multicéntrico randomizado de 508 pacientes en donde se utilizaron 4 esquemas de manejo (1) monoterapia secuencial de csDMARDs, (2) terapia combinada de csDMARDs secuencia, (3) combinación de GC alta dósis+ csDMARDs, (4) TNFi. Se demostró que el esquema de manejo (3) y (4) presentaban una mejoría mas rápida en comparación con los esquemas (1) y (2) al año, sin presentar diferencia en efectos adversos.
    - **t-REACH:** estudio comparativo de eficacia de MTX + SSZ + HCQ + (PDN 15 mg con tapering a 2.5 mg en 10 semanas) VS MTP 120 mg o Triamcinolona 80 mg IM por 1 vez al inicio. Sin diferencias en HAQ, DAS, RADAI a 1 año de seguimiento
    - **CARDERA**: Aleatorio de 467 pacientes con AR precoz a usar MTX sólo, MTX + Csa, MTX + PDN 60 mg y MTX + Csa+PDN 60 mg con tapering rápido en 6 semanas a 7.5 mg para suspender a la semana 34. Se encontró mejores resultados en grupo MTX + PDN en DAS28, Rx,HAQ en seguimiento a 2 años.
    - **CAMERA II** (2012): se comparó MTX + GC VS MTX sólo a 24 semanas. El grupo MTX + GC 72% presentaron remisión a 6 meses (MTX sólo lo presentó en un 61%), necesidad de ADA fue un 30% en MTX sólo vs 16% en MTX + PDN. También se presentaron menos complicaciones en este grupo, lo que se postuló es que el grupo con GC utilizó menos dosis de MTX.
- ***bDMARDs vs tsDMARDs**: poseen similar eficiencia. Existen estudios que comparan en no inferioridad el tofacitinib + MTX VS ABA+MTX, sin diferencias. Dentro de las alternativas de tsDMARDs se encientra el tofacitinib, baricitinib, upacitinib (en fase III) en donde se destaca mayor riesgo de herpes diseminado y mayor riesgo trombótico en perfil de pacientes con riesgo trombótico de base (obesos).*

*La recomendación al respecto es que se elija entre bDMARDs VS tsDMARDs según las características de los pacientes, contraindicaciones, las preferencias de los pacientes y los costos.*

*Los bDMARDs en una revisión sistemática del 2016 se demostró que es mejor que los csDMARDs en caso de falla de estos previa, por lo que se recomienda la adición de los bDMARDs en estos casos. En caso de falla de un bDMARDs lo que se recomeinda el el switch a otro bDMARDs (no está demostrado el cambio de familia en caso de falla). El uso de nuevos bDMARDs y biosimilares han demostrado efectividad,*

*También se ha demostrado el espaciamiento y dismunición de dosis en caso de mantener remisión por un periodo de al menos 2 años.*

*No existe evidencia de que en falla de un csSMARDs se deba pasar a bDMARDs o tsDMARDs en caso de ausencia de los factores de riesgo.*

*La combinación de un csDMARDs a un bDMARDs o tsDMARDs ha mostrado mejor efectividad con menos inmunogenicidad comparado con la monoterapia,*

*El Tofacitinib y el Sarilumab como monoterapia es mejor que el ADA como monoterapia*

*En caso de falla a bDMARDs que sea TNFi, se debe considerar el cambio de familia. En el 2019 se publicó un estudio multicéntrico en el BMJ que comparó RTX, TCZ y ABA como 2do bDMARDs en pactes con falla a TNFi en 3162 pacientes y se probó la mantención de la terapia a 24 meses; el RTX y TCZ se mantuvo mas que el ADA en estos casos.*

*Un segundo estudio del Jama del 2016 se comparó un no TNFi VS otro TNFi como 2do bDMARDs en 300 pctes en un seguimiento de 52 semanas evaluando DAS 28 a la semana 12, 24 y 52. El resultado que se encontró es que el 2do bDMARDs no TNFi fue mas efectivo en bajar la actividad de la enfermedad y en remisión a 24 semanas (pero no de manera significativa)*

***Objetivos de seguimiento:***

*Lo mejor siempre será la remisión, ya que se ha visto que en pacientes con baja actividad de enfermedad se relacionan a una menor funcionalidad, tienden a tener mas fatiga, dolor, rigidez matinal.*

***Update de manejo de AR EULAR (2020):***

- *Sigue siendo el objetivo la remisión o la mínima actividad de la enfermedad como una alternativa aceptable, la que se debe aplicar según la estrategia T2T. Otros objetivos son la majoría de los síntomas, de la funcionalidad, social, trabajo con la idea de evitar la progresión de la enfermedad, del daño articular del cartílago y del hueso.*
- *Factores de mal pronóstico son la actividad moderada/alta luego de los csDMARDs, PCR/VHS alta, elevado número de articulaciones inflamadas, alto FR/CCP, erosiones precoces, fallo a 2 o mas csDMARDs.*
- *Fases de la AR: Naive a csDMRADs, respuesta insuficienica a csDMARDs, insuficiencia de respuesta a bDMARDs o tsDMARDs. No existe evidencia de diferente grado de respuestas de la AR sólo en base al estadío o duración de la enfermedad (no existe diferencia en respuesta entre una AR precoz vs AR establecida), siendo la diferencia en relación al daño estructural existente al retrasar el tratamiento.*
- *MTX debe ser iniciado una vez que se confirme el diagnóstico. El objetivo es lograr una mejoría del 50% a los 3 meses, estos son los pacientes que tienen mas probabilidad de lograr objetivo a los 6 meses de tratamiento. En caso de no observar esta respuesta a los 3 meses (la probabilidad de respeusta a los 6 meses es menor) o de no poder lograr una baja en los GC por inflamación mantenida, se debe ajustar la terapia.*

- *En caso de no lograr la meta con el 1er csDMARDs y no exieste la presencia de factores de riesgo, se puede pasara a otro csDMARDs. En caso de que presente factores de mal pronóstico se puede pasar a bDMARDs o tsDMARDs (sin diferencia entre ellos)*

***En los estudios de imágenes:***

- ***ECO:** En la revisión EULAR de actividad de la enfermedad no incluye a las imágenes copmo seguimiento de tratamiento. Una revisión del 2016 en Join Bone Spine mostró la utilidad del ECO en el diagnóstico precoz de la AR. También en la utilidad en el seguimiento y la detección de la sinovitis subclínica (pero no existe consenso universal de la clasificaicón para darle significancia a los hallazgos subclínicos dado que el seguimiento del tratamiento con ECO no mejora la estrategia de manejo en AR, no es mejor que el exámen físico). En este mismo estudio se objetivó que entre un 25-50% de los pacientes en remisión por DAS-28 presentaron sinovitis al ECO. Las articulaciones dolorosas (que clínicamente impresionan como inflamadas) no se correlacionan con los hallazgos en la ECO, en cambio las articulaciones hincradas sin se relacionan a sinovitis (por power doppler y presencia de derrame articular). **Inhibidores de JAK kinasa**

Las quinasas JAK (Janus quinasa) son un grupo de enzimas (JAK1, JAK2, JAK3 y TYK2) que participan en la señalización de varias citoquinas esenciales para el proceso inflamatorio. Estas citoquinas se unen a receptores en la superficie de las células inmunitarias, activando las JAK y estimulando la transcripción de genes proinflamatorios a través de la vía JAK-STAT (transductores de señal y activadores de la transcripción). Los inhibidores de JAK interfieren con esta vía de señalización, disminuyendo la producción de citoquinas proinflamatorias, lo que reduce la inflamación y el daño articular en la AR.

1. **Tofacitinib** (Xeljanz)
    - **Mecanismo de acción**: Inhibe JAK1, JAK2 y JAK3, bloqueando la señalización de múltiples citoquinas, incluidas IL-6, IL-2 e interferón.
    - **Dosis**: 5 mg por vía oral dos veces al día (caja de 56 comp) o 11 mg de liberación prolongada una vez al día (caja 30 comp). Precio de lista Pfizer: 850.000.
    - **Reacciones adversas**: Infecciones (infecciones respiratorias, herpes zóster), alteraciones hematológicas (neutropenia, anemia), aumento del riesgo de trombosis y efectos gastrointestinales.
2. **Baricitinib** (Olumiant)
    - **Mecanismo de acción**: Inhibe principalmente JAK1 y JAK2, bloqueando la señalización de IL-6 y el interferón gamma.
    - **Dosis**: 2 mg por vía oral una vez al día. Puede considerarse una dosis de 4 mg en algunos pacientes con AR de moderada a grave, siempre con seguimiento cercano. Precio Tecnofarma: 660.000.
    - **Reacciones adversas**: Infecciones graves, trombosis venosa profunda, incremento en el riesgo de herpes zóster, y alteraciones en los lípidos (aumento del colesterol).
3. **Upadacitinib** (Rinvoq)
    - **Mecanismo de acción**: Inhibidor selectivo de JAK1, bloquea la señalización de IL-6, IL-12, IL-23, entre otras citoquinas.
    - **Dosis**: 15 mg una vez al día.
    - **Reacciones adversas**: Infecciones serias, neutropenia, tromboembolismo venoso, elevación de las enzimas hepáticas y riesgo incrementado de eventos cardiovasculares.
4. **Filgotinib** (Jyseleca)
    - **Mecanismo de acción**: Inhibidor selectivo de JAK1, dirigido principalmente contra citoquinas involucradas en la inflamación crónica como IL-6, IL-12, IL-23.
    - **Dosis**: 200 mg una vez al día.
    - **Reacciones adversas**: Infecciones, principalmente del tracto respiratorio, aumento del riesgo de herpes zóster, elevación de enzimas hepáticas y posible riesgo cardiovascular.

RAM

- **Infecciones**: El riesgo de infecciones graves, como infecciones del tracto respiratorio superior y herpes zóster, es mayor. Los pacientes deben ser evaluados para tuberculosis y otras infecciones latentes antes de iniciar el tratamiento.
- **Alteraciones hematológicas**: Neutropenia, anemia y trombocitopenia han sido observadas. Se recomienda realizar un monitoreo regular del hemograma completo.
- **Tromboembolismo venoso**: Se ha reportado un mayor riesgo de trombosis venosa profunda y embolia pulmonar, especialmente en pacientes con factores de riesgo.
- **Hepatotoxicidad**: Elevación de las enzimas hepáticas, por lo que se debe realizar monitoreo de función hepática durante el tratamiento.
- **Lípidos**: Aumento del colesterol total y LDL en algunos pacientes.

### Riesgo de neoplasias:

No hay evidencia de que sDMARD, RTX, anti TNF, aumente el cáncer sólido o de piel.

El Tofacitinib tendrían un  riesgo de neoplasias nuevas o recurrentes. No se sabe si es un efecto de clase

**AR y Vacunas**

MTX y Rituximab bajan la actividad de vacunas.

**Cirugía en Artritis**

Objetivos: aliviar dolor, mantener función.

Opciones:

- Sinoviectomía bajo artroscopía.
- Reparación de tendones
- Reemplazo total de la articulación
- Solución de complicaciones nerviosas

### Protocolo Ley Ricarte Soto 2019

Medicamentos disponibles

- Etanercept
- Abatacept
- Adalimumab
- Golimumab SC (EV no está en LRS)
- Tocilizumab SC (EV no está en LRS)
- Tofacitinib
- Rituximab
- Baricitinib (desde 2025)
- Upadacitinib (desde 2025)

El inicio del fármaco debe ser dentro de los 60 días desde solicitud

**Confirmación Diagnóstica y Criterios de inclusión**
Criterios de clasificación de EULAR/ACR 2010

***Artritis Reumatoide activa refractaria a tratamiento habitual***
Para ser considerados, debe cumplirse actividad y refractariedad.

Activa: 

1. ***DAS28-VHS sobre 5,1***
2. ***6 o más dolorosas, 3 o más hinchadas, VHS >30, Rigidez >45 min.*** 
    
    ***En 2 evaluaciones*** con diferencia entre si entre ***30 y 120 días***. Siendo la última no más de 30 días antes de subir a la plataforma.
    

Refractaria: 

Activa pese a uso de 3 FARMES, incluyendo 2 de los siguientes: MTX, LEF, SSZ en dosis máximas por un periodo de 5 meses o menos si no mala tolerancia.

Criterios de exclusión:

- Otra enfermedad autoinmune
- Infección activa
- Cáncer en los últimos 5 años, salvo piel no melanoma
- TBC activa
- Embarazo
- Contraindicación de los fármacos incluidos

Requerimiento previo a tratamiento

- TBC activa o latante. PPD o IGRA.
- Anti neumocócica (obligatoria)
- Anti influenza anual
- VIH ELISA, VHB (superficie y core) y VHC
- Radiografia de manos al inicio y a los 12 meses
- Evaluar riesgo de Herpes Zoster
- Test de embarazo si corresponde
- 2 métodos de control de embarazo

**Tratamientos**

- Primera línea:
    - Anti TNF:
        - Etanercept 50 mg SC cada 7 días
        - Adalimumab 40 mg SC cada 14 días
        - Golimumab 50 mg SC cada 30 días
    - Abatacept 125 mg SC cada 7 días
    - Tocilizumab 162 mg SC cada semana
    - Tofacitinib 5 mg cada 12 horas VO
- Segunda línea
    - Rituximab (primera línea con justitificación) 1 g 0 y 15 días EV, nueva infusión según respuesta, nunca antes de 6 meses.
    
    Cadena de frío para parenterales: entre 2 y 8°C
    

***Criterios de continuidad de tratamiento***

1. Los mismos de inclusión
2. Documentar respuesta al alcanzar remisión o baja actividad
3. En AR de larga data, si el tratante considera que ha sido eficaz

***Criterios de cambio de fármaco***

1. Fracaso de biológico: uso por 6 meses con 2 evaluaciones por reumatólogo, no alcanza reducción de 0,6 en las mediciones del DAS28 o no alcanza actividad baja o remisión 
    
    Respuesta según reducción de DAS28:
    
    Respuesta clínica significativa >1,2
    
    Respuesta clínica moderada 0,6 a 1,2
    
    Sin respuesta <0,6
    

Seguimiento

Cada 3 meses por 2 veces, luego semestral.
El médico tratante será el encargado de enviar el Formulario de Notificación de Reacciones Adversas al ISP y, paralelamente, deberá enviar copia de dicho formulario al Comité de Expertos Clínicos del Prestador Aprobado, a través de la plataforma electrónica del FONASA para la ley Nº20.850
